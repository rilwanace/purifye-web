“I have two tablets on the counter — one for PickMe Food, one for Uber Eats — and I’m also getting WhatsApp orders. I missed three orders yesterday.”
“The kitchen is screaming, the printer is beeping on both tablets, and I’m manually calling out orders. Something is going to break.”

Managing orders across two delivery platforms plus WhatsApp plus dine-in is the number one daily operational pain reported by Sri Lankan restaurant operators. There is no perfect solution — but there is a system that makes it survivable, and a technology roadmap to make it smooth.

01
Acknowledge the problem: two tablets is chaos by design

Running PickMe Food and Uber Eats simultaneously means two separate tablet interfaces, two separate order streams, two separate acceptance timers, and two separate call centres if something goes wrong. This is the stated number one operational pain among Colombo-area restaurant operators (A5 research, 2024).

The global solution is a delivery aggregator — software that combines all platform orders into one interface. Global players like UrbanPiper exist but are not established in Sri Lanka as of 2024. The local solution is a designed workflow, not a technology fix.

Until a local aggregator exists, the right response is: assign one person specifically to manage both tablets. Not the cashier. Not the kitchen. A dedicated order-management role during peak hours. Even if that person is the owner, making it an explicit, dedicated role reduces errors by 60–70%.

Two platforms need one person watching them. Don’t split the job across three people who also have other jobs.

02
Create a single printed order sheet system

Every incoming order — from any platform or channel — goes onto one printed or written order ticket before it enters the kitchen. The ticket has: order number, items, platform, and time received.

This sounds obvious. Almost no small Sri Lankan restaurant does it. The kitchen sees three simultaneous orders (one from each platform plus dine-in) without any unified view of priority or sequence. Errors are inevitable.

If you don’t have a receipt printer: use a lined notebook on the pass. Tear out the page when the order goes out. A torn page = completed order. A page still there = order pending. It costs nothing and it works.

All orders into one system before they enter the kitchen. This is the entire solution.

03
Set delivery platforms to ‘scheduled’ during your rush hour

Both PickMe Food and Uber Eats allow you to set a preparation time per order. During your lunch rush, set your prep time to 20–25 minutes. This gives the kitchen breathing room and reduces the number of cancelled orders due to delays.

You can also temporarily pause incoming orders on one or both platforms if your kitchen is overwhelmed. Use this deliberately — not as an emergency measure, but as a planned tool for your busiest 30-minute window.

PickMe Food driver shortages are a noted problem (multiple restaurant owner reports). A driver who doesn’t arrive adds a cold order sitting under heat lamps to your kitchen backlog. Setting realistic prep times reduces the mismatch between when food is ready and when drivers arrive.

Your prep time setting is a traffic management tool. Use it like one.

04
Build a WhatsApp order workflow with a fixed menu and response template

WhatsApp orders can be efficient or they can be chaos, depending on whether you have a system. The system: a pinned message in WhatsApp Business that lists today’s menu and an order format.

Every customer who orders via WhatsApp uses the format: “NAME / ITEM / QUANTITY / DELIVERY ADDRESS or PICKUP.” That gives you everything you need to process the order without a back-and-forth conversation.

Set up WhatsApp Business with an auto-reply to new messages: “Hi! Thanks for messaging [Restaurant]. Today’s menu: [link or list]. To order, please send: Your name / items / quantity / pickup or delivery address. We’ll confirm in 5 minutes.”

This reduces your order-taking time per WhatsApp order from 5–10 minutes to 60 seconds.

05
Set up a basic kitchen display or call-out system

In a restaurant doing 30+ delivery orders per lunch hour, verbal call-out from the counter to the kitchen is the single biggest source of errors. A basic kitchen display system (KDS) solves this.

NooDelo (noodelo.com) is a Sri Lankan POS provider offering QR code table ordering and a Kitchen Display System. Pricing is not publicly listed but they offer a free demo. For a restaurant doing high delivery + dine-in volume simultaneously, a KDS pays for itself in error reduction within months.

If a full KDS is out of budget: print tickets at the pass. A simple receipt printer (Rs.8,000–15,000 one-time) connected to the order tablet routes all orders to a kitchen printer. Both PickMe Food and Uber Eats support printer integration via third-party apps.

A kitchen that reads printed tickets makes fewer errors than a kitchen that hears shouted orders.

06
Review platform payouts weekly — disputes cost you money

Delivery platform settlements come weekly, but they are frequently disputed. Restaurants report: cancelled orders still charged, refund deductions without notification, promotional discount deductions that exceed agreed terms.

Review your PickMe Food and Uber Eats weekly settlement reports line by line. Flag discrepancies immediately via the merchant support portal (don’t wait until next month). Keep a simple spreadsheet: expected payout vs. actual payout, with notes on any variance.

Consumers have reported PickMe Food payment and refund problems lasting 6 weeks with multiple escalations. Restaurants face the same. The platforms have structural leverage — but they will correct documented errors if you catch them and escalate promptly.

The platform will not audit its own errors. You have to. Weekly, not monthly.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Design your order system
Designate one person as ‘order manager’ during peak hours. Define their role explicitly.
Create a standard order ticket format: platform / order number / items / time received.
Set your delivery platform prep times to realistic values (20 min minimum during lunch rush).
Set up WhatsApp Business auto-reply with your order format template.
2–3 hrs
week2
Unify your kitchen flow
If you don’t have a ticket printer: buy one. Use it for all incoming orders.
Implement: one ticket per order, all orders unified before entering kitchen.
Test for 5 days. Note: how many missed orders? How many late orders?
Brief kitchen staff on the new system — every order is a ticket, every ticket is a commitment.
1 hr setup + 5 days practice
week3
Audit your platforms
Pull last month’s settlement reports from both PickMe Food and Uber Eats.
Compare total orders vs. total payout. Flag any cancelled orders that were charged.
Set a weekly 15-minute calendar reminder: ‘review platform payouts.’
Optimise your delivery menu: remove your slowest 3 items from both platforms.
2–3 hrs
week4
Technology decision
Book a demo with NooDelo if order volume justifies a KDS investment.
Calculate: how many order errors per week × average order value = monthly error cost.
If error cost exceeds Rs.10,000/month, a kitchen display system has clear ROI.
Assess: is a dedicated order-management hire justified at current volume?
2 hrs

Order chaos is a systems problem, not a people problem. The right system makes the same people dramatically more effective.

Two tablets, WhatsApp, and dine-in simultaneously is not a scalable model without a unified order system. The technology will catch up — until then, the right process is your solution.

Start today: assign a specific person to watch both delivery tablets during tomorrow’s lunch rush. That’s step one. Everything else builds from there.
