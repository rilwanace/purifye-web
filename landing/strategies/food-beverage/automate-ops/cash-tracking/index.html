“At the end of every day I count the cash and it never matches what I think we sold. I don’t know if it’s theft, errors, or if I’m just counting wrong.”
“I have no idea what my actual profit is. I know money comes in and goes out but I couldn’t tell you if I’m making anything.”

Most small Sri Lankan F&B operators run entirely on cash with no bookkeeping, no P&L, and no idea what their actual profit margin is. This isn’t laziness — it’s the norm. But it means you can’t make good decisions, spot theft, or plan for your slow months. Here’s how to fix it without becoming an accountant.

01
Start a daily cash sheet — tonight, not next week

The simplest financial system for a small restaurant: a lined notebook, one page per day. Three columns: Sales IN (by category if possible), Cash OUT (every purchase and payment), Closing Balance.

Do this every day at closing. It takes 10 minutes. After 30 days you have enough data to know your average daily revenue, your average daily spend, and whether there is a consistent gap between what you should have and what you actually have.

Most small operators in Sri Lanka track revenue in physical notebooks or basic mobile notes with no P&L. The daily cash sheet doesn’t need to be fancy — it needs to be consistent. A consistent imperfect system beats a perfect system you never start.

You can’t manage what you don’t measure. The daily cash sheet is your financial foundation.

02
Separate your delivery platform revenue from your cash revenue

Delivery platform revenue (PickMe Food, Uber Eats) is paid weekly, not daily. This creates a timing mismatch that confuses cash flow: you served 50 delivery orders this week, but you haven’t received that money yet.

Track them separately. In your daily cash sheet: (1) Cash/card sales today. (2) Delivery orders today (value, not yet received). When your weekly platform settlement arrives, mark it received and reconcile against your running total.

Delivery platform settlements frequently have discrepancies: cancelled orders, promotional deductions, refunds. Reconciling weekly means you catch errors within days, not months. Restaurant owners report refund disputes taking 6 weeks+ if not escalated promptly.

Cash in hand today and delivery revenue on its way are two different things. Track them separately.

03
Install a basic POS — even the cheapest one saves hours

Sri Lanka has several affordable POS solutions designed for the local market: BigPOS (bigpos.lk), POSLK (poslk.com), POS System SL (possystem.lk, from Rs.25,000), Sellmo (sellmopos.com), and SmartPOS. A basic POS automatically generates end-of-day sales reports, tracks which items sold, and reduces manual cash errors.

The adoption gap is real: despite availability, most small-to-medium F&B operators in Sri Lanka use paper or nothing. Digital literacy and one-time cost are the main barriers.

You don’t need the most expensive system. You need one that: records every sale, generates a daily total, and lets you see your top-selling items. All the local providers above offer this at entry-level pricing.

A Rs.25,000 POS that saves 1 hour per day of manual reconciliation pays for itself in 3 months.

04
Track your top 5 cost categories weekly

You don’t need to track every rupee spent — you need to track the five things that eat most of your money. For most Sri Lankan F&B businesses: (1) Ingredients/produce. (2) Staff wages. (3) Rent. (4) Utilities (electricity + gas). (5) Delivery platform commissions.

Write these five numbers down every week. Post-crisis Sri Lanka has seen dramatic cost volatility: electricity bills are the highest in South Asia (PublicFinance.lk, 2024); ingredient prices swung violently in 2022–2023. If you’re not tracking these five numbers, you won’t notice when they quietly eat your margins.

A simple spreadsheet (Google Sheets, free) with 5 rows and 52 columns (one per week) gives you a full-year cost view in 10 minutes of setup. You’ll see seasonal patterns, spot anomalies, and have real data when your next price review is due.

Five numbers, tracked weekly. That is enough to run a small food business with clear financial visibility.

05
Separate your personal and business finances — today

Mixing personal and business finances is the single most common financial mistake among Sri Lankan sole proprietors and family businesses. When personal expenses are paid from business cash, you lose the ability to measure your actual business profit.

The fix is a separate bank account for the business. All business revenue goes in. All business expenses come out. Once a month, transfer your ‘profit’ to your personal account. Everything left in the business account is your operating buffer.

You don’t need a business bank account for this initially — a second personal account that you treat as strictly business will work. The discipline is in the separation, not the account type. Once you have a separate account, your actual business profit becomes visible for the first time.

If you can’t see your profit, you can’t manage it. Separation is the first step.

06
Build a 3-month operating reserve — even if it’s small

Seasonal slumps, equipment failures, slow delivery platform settlements, and unexpected cost spikes (the 2022 crisis is the extreme example) can destroy a food business that has no financial buffer.

Target: 3 months of fixed costs (rent + staff + utilities) in a separate savings account. For most small operators, this might be Rs.200,000–500,000. Build toward it by moving 5–10% of monthly profit there until you reach it.

During the 2022 crisis, operators who had a cash buffer survived the ingredient price surge. Wish Bakers (15 outlets) absorbed tripled operating costs by drawing on reserves and built fierce loyalty by holding prices. Those without a buffer closed.

A 3-month reserve is not a luxury for a food business in Sri Lanka. It’s the difference between surviving a bad quarter and closing.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Start tracking tonight
Buy a lined notebook. Create your first daily cash sheet page tonight at closing.
Three columns: Sales In / Cash Out / Closing Balance. Fill it in.
Separate your delivery revenue from your cash revenue in the sheet.
Do this every day for the next 30 days without exception.
10 min/day
week2
Separate business and personal
Open a separate bank account for your business (or designate an existing account strictly for business use).
All business income goes into this account only.
All business expenses come from this account only.
Transfer your salary/profit to your personal account once a month.
2–3 hrs (bank visit)
week3
Install a POS or build the spreadsheet
Book a demo with one of the local POS providers (BigPOS, POSLK, POS System SL).
If no POS yet: set up a Google Sheets weekly cost tracker with your 5 main cost categories.
Enter last month’s costs from memory/receipts. Start tracking this week.
Reconcile your delivery platform settlements against your daily order tracker this week.
3–4 hrs
week4
Build your financial picture
After 30 days of daily cash sheets: calculate your average daily revenue.
Calculate your average daily cost. Subtract to find your average daily profit.
Identify your worst-performing week in the 30-day period. What caused it?
Set a target: 3 months of fixed costs in a savings buffer. Start transferring 5% of monthly profit monthly.
2 hrs (monthly review)
After 4 weeks, you'll have:
✓
30 days of daily cash sheets completed without gaps
✓
Business and personal finances fully separated
✓
A 5-category weekly cost tracker running in Google Sheets
✓
Delivery platform settlements reconciled weekly
✓
A financial buffer account opened and funded, even if small
You now know what your business is actually doing. That knowledge is the foundation of every other improvement.

Most small Sri Lankan F&B businesses have no idea if they’re profitable. Not because they don’t want to know — but because the system to find out doesn’t exist yet. You’ve just built it.

Start tonight: buy a notebook and fill in your first daily cash sheet before you close. Revenue in, expenses out, closing balance. That’s it. Do it every day.
