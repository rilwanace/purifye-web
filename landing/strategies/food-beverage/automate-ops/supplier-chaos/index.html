“Every morning I’m on the phone to four different suppliers negotiating prices that change every single day. It eats an hour before I even start cooking.”
“Half the time the delivery doesn’t come when they said it would, or the quality is wrong, and I have no leverage to do anything about it.”

Manning Market prices change daily. Suppliers deliver late, substitute items without notice, and charge inconsistent prices. Most small Sri Lankan restaurant owners treat this as a fact of life. It is — but there’s a system to make it far less chaotic.

01
Build a preferred supplier list with at least 2 options per category

The morning market dependency is a defining feature of the Sri Lankan food business. Manning Market (Pettah, Colombo) and local wholesale markets set daily prices based on harvest, season, and import availability. Menu decisions flex daily based on what’s available at what price.

The problem: most small operators have one supplier per category. If that supplier is late, raises prices, or runs out, there’s no immediate fallback. Build your list to 2 suppliers per category (produce, meat/poultry, fish, dry goods). You won’t use both every day — but having the second option gives you negotiating leverage with the first.

Post-crisis, chicken prices swung from Rs.400/kg to Rs.2,100/kg in a single year. Operators with multiple meat suppliers navigated this better than those locked into a single source. The second supplier is your insurance policy.

One supplier per category is a single point of failure. Two is a supply chain.

02
Consolidate your supplier communications onto WhatsApp

Most supplier negotiations still happen over phone calls — a time-intensive, unrecorded, easy-to-dispute channel. Move everything to WhatsApp.

A WhatsApp message to each supplier every evening: “Tomorrow’s order: [item / quantity]. Please confirm price and delivery time.” The supplier confirms in writing. You have a record. Disputes are resolved in seconds.

The 10-minute daily supplier WhatsApp round takes the place of the 1-hour phone negotiation session. You also build a daily log of what you ordered, at what price, and when it arrived — which is invaluable when a supplier tries to overcharge you or you need to compare prices between alternatives.

COPY-PASTE THIS — Daily supplier order message
Hi [supplier name]. Tomorrow’s order from [Restaurant]: [item 1] — [quantity]. [item 2] — [quantity]. [item 3] — [quantity]. Please confirm price per kg/unit and delivery time. Thank you.

Written orders, confirmed in writing. Three minutes per supplier, per day.

03
Track daily ingredient prices in a simple log

No tool in Sri Lanka currently tracks daily wholesale prices against historical data. So build your own — in a notebook or Google Sheet. After each supplier confirmation: log the date, item, quantity, and price per unit. After 4 weeks you have a pricing baseline.

This baseline tells you: what is the normal price range for chicken this season? Is today’s price from Supplier A high, low, or average? When is the cheapest time to buy in bulk for non-perishables?

Post-crisis price volatility made this essential. Ingredient costs that move 30–50% in a week (as they did during 2022–2023) destroy margins for operators who aren’t watching. Four weeks of price tracking gives you enough data to know when to switch menus and when to buy ahead.

Historical price data gives you leverage. Your supplier knows you’re watching.

04
Create a ‘weather menu’ — a daily flex based on what’s cheap

Your best defence against ingredient price volatility is a menu that can flex. A ‘weather menu’ or ‘market menu’ approach: your core 5–8 items are fixed. Your daily specials (2–3 items) are decided each morning based on what’s available at the best price.

This is not a weakness — it’s a strength. “Today’s special: fresh catch from the market this morning” is a compelling selling point, not an apology.

The daily special announcement via WhatsApp broadcast (“Today’s special: [dish], Rs.[price], limited portions”) creates urgency, drives foot traffic, and uses your freshest, cheapest-today ingredient. Kottu roti itself was historically invented as a way to use day-old godamba roti — the entire category is a waste-utilisation innovation that became premium.

The best restaurants in Sri Lanka turn ‘what’s cheap today’ into ‘what’s special today.’

05
Negotiate credit terms with your most reliable supplier

Cash-on-delivery is the default for most small Sri Lankan restaurant suppliers. But operators who have built a reliable payment track record with a supplier can negotiate 7–14 day credit terms.

Credit terms transform your daily cash flow: instead of paying cash for Rs.15,000 of produce every morning, you settle the week’s account on Friday. This smooths the cash flow mismatch between your daily purchasing and your weekly delivery platform settlements.

The conversation: “I’ve been buying from you for [X months/years] and paying on time. I’d like to discuss 7-day credit terms on my weekly account. My average weekly order is Rs.[amount].” Not every supplier will agree. Some will. The ones who do become preferred partners.

Cash in the morning is a supplier’s preference. Credit terms are your working capital advantage.

06
Consolidate dry goods purchasing to once a week

Non-perishables (rice, flour, spices, oil, lentils) don’t need to be purchased daily. Consolidating dry goods to one weekly purchase from one reliable supplier saves: 2–3 daily supplier calls, daily travel to market, and the impulse-buy waste that happens when you’re at the market anyway.

Calculate your weekly dry goods usage. Order 1.2x that amount (20% buffer for unexpected demand). Set a fixed day and time for the weekly dry goods delivery. Don’t deviate from it.

The morning market trip is unavoidable for fresh produce. But combining fresh produce buying with dry goods buying doubles your market time unnecessarily. Reserve the morning market for fresh items only. Everything else: scheduled weekly delivery.

Your time at the market is for fresh produce decisions. Everything else should arrive on schedule.
