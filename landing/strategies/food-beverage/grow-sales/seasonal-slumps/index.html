“August–September is always a disaster. The bills don’t stop but the customers do.”
“During Vesak week I can’t even sell meat and the Dansal stalls outside are giving food away for free. The whole week is gone.”

Every F&B business in Sri Lanka has slow months. The difference between owners who survive them and owners who don’t is whether they planned for the drop and built revenue alternatives before the slump arrived — not after.

01
Map your full revenue calendar — then plan against it

Sri Lanka has predictable F&B demand cycles. Peak periods: Sinhala/Tamil New Year (April), Ramadan/Iftar season (April–May), December Christmas & New Year (prices spike 30–50%), November–March wedding season (south/west coast), Valentine’s Day (February), corporate year-end (November–December).

Dead periods: May–June (SW monsoon onset), August–September (mid-year quiet), post-Avurudu lull (mid-April). Vesak full moon day: alcohol and meat sales banned by government order. ~12 Poya days per year: alcohol banned.

Draw a 12-month revenue calendar right now. Mark your last year’s actual monthly revenue. If you don’t have records, estimate. The pattern you see is real and it repeats every year. Plan your staffing, purchases, and promotions against this map — not against last month.

A slow month is only a crisis if it surprises you. Plan for it and it becomes a scheduled maintenance period.

02
Build a B2B or corporate lunch package for off-peak months

Companies like SuperServ demonstrate the scale achievable in institutional catering: 120,000 meals/day, 50+ corporate clients across 12+ districts. You don’t need their scale. Landing 2–3 corporate lunch contracts for the CBD office buildings near you can smooth your cash flow dramatically during dead retail months.

Post-crisis office return in Colombo’s CBD (Port City, World Trade Centre area) is driving new demand for corporate lunch. A daily lunch package of Rs.600–900 per head, delivered to 30 office workers, is Rs.18,000–27,000 per day in predictable revenue.

How to reach corporate clients: LinkedIn to HR managers and office admins, direct WhatsApp to office buildings near you, a simple one-page menu PDF with pricing and minimum order quantity. Start with one client on a 2-week trial. Nail the consistency. Then expand.

COPY-PASTE THIS — Corporate lunch outreach message
Hi [Name], I run [restaurant name] near your office at [location]. We offer daily office lunch packages starting from Rs.[price]/head, minimum [X] pax. Can I send you our menu? Happy to do a free trial delivery this week.

Corporate lunch is a counter-cyclical revenue stream. When retail is quiet, offices are full.

03
Launch seasonal menus 3 weeks before every peak period

Most restaurants prepare for Ramadan, Christmas, and Avurudu about 3 days before they happen. The best operators launch their Iftar menu in March, their Christmas set menu in November, and their Avurudu package in late March — and promote them aggressively on WhatsApp and social media.

Sri Lanka’s Iftar market is substantial: 13+ major Colombo establishments run dedicated Ramadan programmes, with Iftar buffet pricing from Rs.2,900 to Rs.9,000/head. Even a small restaurant can run a Rs.1,500–2,500 Iftar package and fill tables every evening.

Valentine’s Day (February 14) drives high demand for couples’ dining. Yamu.lk publishes a “romantic dinner guide” annually — get featured by emailing them 4 weeks in advance with your special menu.

Wedding season (November–March, south/west coast) is the biggest catering revenue window of the year for caterers. Bookings happen 12–18 months in advance. If you’re not marketing catering packages in April for the following wedding season, you’re already behind.

The best time to promote your Christmas menu is early November. The best time to promote your Iftar menu is mid-March.

04
Shift to delivery-heavy operations during monsoon months

Colombo and the SW region experience heavy rains from May–September. Foot traffic drops during heavy rain. But delivery demand rises — homebound customers order in. This is a direct swap: use monsoon months to aggressively promote your delivery channel.

Invest the slow foot-traffic period into improving your delivery listing photos, launching WhatsApp direct orders, and running platform promotions that increase your delivery visibility.

SriDelivery operates specifically in south coast towns (Galle, Weligama, Mirissa, Matara, Negombo). If you operate in these areas, register on SriDelivery in addition to PickMe Food and Uber Eats — platform diversity reduces your risk when one platform has a driver shortage.

When it rains, Sri Lankans order in. Make sure your delivery listing is the one they find.

05
Use slow months to invest in your team and your processes

Dead months are the only time you have space to do the things that make peak months more profitable: staff training, recipe standardisation, menu engineering, equipment maintenance, and systems setup.

The hospitality sector loses 20–25% of its workforce annually. Operators who use slow months to train, promote internal talent, and create clear career paths retain staff better than those who treat everyone as interchangeable.

Slow month checklist: standardise your 5 best-selling recipes in writing. Deep-clean and service your kitchen equipment. Train your most promising staff member to handle a specific area (front of house, social media, ordering). Refresh your menu: remove slow sellers, add one new item.

A slow month that you use well is an investment in your peak months. A slow month you waste is just lost revenue.

06
Negotiate rent relief — most landlords will deal during dead months

Your rent doesn’t drop during your slow months, but it can if you ask. Sri Lankan landlords for commercial food premises are not immune to the reality that their tenant leaving costs them more than a month of reduced rent.

The ask: a 2-month rent reduction during your proven dead period (provide your actual monthly revenue records as evidence), offset against a slight increase during your peak months, or a simple fixed discount for the year.

Not every landlord will agree. But most small-restaurant landlords in Sri Lanka are individuals, not corporations, and a respectful, data-supported conversation about seasonality is worth having. The worst they can say is no — and you’re no worse off than you were.

Fixed costs in a seasonal business are the primary cause of owner distress. Reduce them wherever you can.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Map and plan
Draw your 12-month revenue map using last year’s actual (or estimated) monthly revenue.
Identify your 2 best peak months and your 2 worst dead months.
List every Sri Lankan public holiday and seasonal event that affects your customer flow.
Decide: which peak event will you prepare a special menu for first?
3–4 hrs
week2
Build your off-season revenue streams
Identify 3 office buildings or companies within 2km of your restaurant.
Prepare a 1-page corporate lunch menu with pricing and minimum order.
Send the first WhatsApp message to HR contacts or office admins this week.
Prepare your first seasonal menu (next big seasonal event) and set the launch date 3 weeks out.
4–6 hrs
week3
Use the next slow month productively
Write down your top 5 recipes properly. Standardise and photograph them.
Service and clean your kitchen equipment during the quiet week.
Train one staff member on one new skill (social media, ordering, customer service).
Refine your delivery menu and update your listing photos.
Full slow week
week4
Rent negotiation and financial review
Pull your monthly revenue data for the last 12 months.
Calculate your revenue/cost gap during your worst 2 months.
Request a meeting with your landlord. Present the data. Ask for seasonal flexibility.
Calculate: if you landed one 30-person corporate lunch account, what would that add monthly?
2–4 hrs

Seasonality is not your problem. Running a seasonal business without a seasonal plan is the problem. You’ve fixed that.
