Strategies›Food & Beverage›Reduce Costs & Waste›Overordering, wasting food, don’t know actual costs
“By Friday there’s always produce in the fridge that we didn’t use. I throw it away every week and it makes me sick.”
“I don’t actually know what each dish costs me. I just know I’m losing money somewhere.”

Sri Lanka wastes 110 tonnes of food per day from restaurants in the Colombo district alone. Most of that waste starts with overordering — buying more than you can use because you don’t track what you consume. The fix is simpler than most owners expect: basic tracking, smaller orders, and flexible menus.

01
Calculate your actual food cost per dish

Food cost % = ingredient cost ÷ selling price × 100. Target range for most dishes: 28–35%. Catering has more flexibility (15–30% net margin possible). Cafes with high beverage revenue often run 20–25%.

Start with your 5 best-selling dishes. For each one: write down every ingredient, the quantity used per portion, and the current price per unit. Calculate the total ingredient cost per serving. Compare it to what you’re charging. Most owners are shocked by this number.

Post-crisis ingredient volatility makes this calculation more important than ever. Chicken swung from Rs.400/kg to Rs.2,100/kg between 2021 and 2023. An operator who set prices in 2021 and didn’t recalculate food cost in 2023 was selling at a loss.

If you don’t know your food cost, you don’t know your margin. There is no shortcut around this calculation.

02
Build a simple weekly inventory tracking sheet

You don’t need expensive software. You need a Google Sheet with two lists: ‘opening stock’ and ‘closing stock.’ Once per week (Friday closing is a good time), count what you have. The difference between what you bought and what you used vs. what’s left is your consumption and your waste.

Research on Sri Lankan restaurants confirms: “Most shops face overstock and stock shortage issues.” A weekly count takes 20–30 minutes and shows you immediately where you’re over-buying.

Track perishables (produce, meat, fish) and non-perishables separately. Perishables are your waste risk. When your Friday count shows you always have too much of a specific item, that item’s order quantity drops by that amount next week.

Twenty minutes of counting on Friday saves you the cost of everything you throw away the next Friday.

03
Order to use — not to be safe

The instinct to over-order comes from fear of running out. But in a market where produce goes bad in 2–3 days, a 20% over-order is almost always 20% waste.

The system: track your actual daily usage for each perishable item over 2 weeks. Calculate your average daily usage. Order exactly 1.1x that (10% buffer). If you run out of something midday, that is valuable information: the item is more popular than you thought and deserves a higher order quantity or a dedicated daily special slot.

Smaller, more frequent orders of perishables are almost always cheaper than large orders with high waste. The best operators in Sri Lanka order fresh produce every 1–2 days for items with short shelf life, and every 3–4 days for items with longer shelf life.

Ordering exactly what you need is harder than it sounds. It’s also the single biggest waste reduction lever you have.

04
Use surplus produce in daily specials — not the bin

The most successful waste-reduction strategy in Sri Lanka’s F&B sector is also the most elegant: daily specials built around whatever produce needs to be used that day.

A WhatsApp broadcast at 10am: “Today’s special: [dish], Rs.[price], limited to 20 portions.” The item moves your most-at-risk produce at full price, creates urgency, and drives traffic. Kottu roti itself was invented for this purpose: repurposing day-old godamba roti that couldn’t be sold. The entire category is a waste-reduction innovation that became a national dish.

Keep a ‘Use Today’ tray in your fridge: anything that needs to be used within 24 hours goes there. The morning kitchen brief includes: “What’s in the Use Today tray, and what are we making with it?”

Waste is just an ingredient waiting for the right dish. Build the system to find that dish every day.

05
Implement portion standardisation

Inconsistent portions are a hidden cost. If your rice and curry is sometimes 350g of rice and sometimes 500g, you’re either overserving (wasting ingredient cost) or underserving (losing customers).

Standardise portions using weight or volume measures. A scoop of a known size, a ladle of known volume, a portion container of known capacity. This is especially critical for expensive ingredients (proteins, premium sauces) where even 20g of variance per serving adds up to thousands of rupees per week across all orders.

Photograph each standard portion as the kitchen reference. Post it at the pass. Make it visual, not just numerical. A new cook who has never worked with you can replicate a photographed standard more reliably than a written recipe alone.

Consistent portions protect your food cost and your customer’s expectation simultaneously.

06
Reduce your menu to eliminate slow sellers

Menu engineering: track which items sell fewer than 3 portions per day over a month. Those items are consuming ingredient shelf space, ordering complexity, and prep time for almost no revenue return.

A focused menu of 10–15 items that all sell well delivers: lower ingredient variety (each ingredient is used across multiple dishes, reducing waste), faster kitchen prep, fewer supplier SKUs to manage, and a clearer identity to customers.

The best cloud kitchens in Sri Lanka, including Foodprints by Koluu, are cited for “maintaining menu integrity while minimising waste through selective menu design.” Every item on the menu should earn its place by selling consistently and using shared ingredients.

A smaller menu done brilliantly beats a large menu done poorly. And it costs you less to run.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Know your numbers
Calculate the food cost % for your top 5 selling dishes this week.
Identify which item has the highest food cost % — that is your most urgent pricing or waste problem.
Set up a Google Sheet: items / weekly order quantity / weekly usage / waste.
Count your stock this Friday closing. Use it as your Week 1 baseline.
4–6 hrs
week2
Fix your ordering
Track daily usage of your top 5 perishables for 2 weeks.
Calculate average daily usage for each. Set order quantities to 1.1x average.
Shift perishable ordering to every 1–2 days (smaller, fresher, less waste).
Create a ‘Use Today’ tray in your fridge. Brief kitchen staff on it.
1 hr setup + daily habit
week3
Standardise and simplify
Implement portion standards for your top 5 dishes. Photograph them.
Review your full menu. Identify items selling fewer than 3 portions/day.
Remove the bottom 3 slowest sellers. Consolidate the ingredient orders you no longer need.
Build a daily specials slot specifically for surplus produce.
3–4 hrs
week4
Review and refine
After 4 weeks: how much less produce are you throwing away per week?
Have your food cost %s improved on your tracked dishes?
Which daily specials sold best? Make the best ones permanent.
Re-evaluate your bottom 3 menu items. Have any recovered? Replace those that haven’t.
1 hr (monthly)

✓
Food cost % calculated for your top 5 dishes
✓

✓
Perishable order quantities reduced to match actual usage
✓
A daily specials system using surplus produce
✓
Portion standards photographed and posted in the kitchen
The 110 tonnes wasted daily across Colombo restaurants is everyone’s problem. But your share of it is entirely within your control — and every rupee of waste you eliminate goes straight to your margin.

Food waste is not a moral issue for most small restaurant owners — it’s a financial one. Every kilo you throw away is a kilo you paid for and received nothing in return. The system to stop it takes one week to build.

Start today: calculate the food cost % for your single most popular dish. If it’s above 40%, you have an urgent pricing or waste problem. If it’s below 25%, you might be underpriced.
