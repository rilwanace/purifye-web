Strategies›Food & Beverage›Reduce Costs & Waste›Staff costs rising, people keep leaving
“I trained someone for 3 months and they left for a job in Qatar. I don’t know how to compete with that.”
“The minimum wage went up again. EPF, ETF, all of it. Every month the wage bill gets bigger and the staff don’t stay long enough to justify it.”

Sri Lanka’s hospitality sector loses 20–25% of its workforce annually. The industry needed 24,898 more employees in 2024 than it had. You cannot stop the migration pull to the Middle East, Australia, or Europe. What you can do is build a retention system that keeps your best people longer than your competitors keep theirs.

01
Understand why your staff actually leave

The research is clear on SL hospitality staff departure reasons: (1) Overseas migration for 3–4x the salary. (2) Irregular hours and split shifts. (3) Perceived low social status of the work. (4) No visible career progression path.

You cannot match a Qatar salary. You can address items 2, 3, and 4. Ask every departing staff member directly: “What would have made you stay?” Ask every current employee: “What would make you leave?” The answers will tell you where your controllable retention problem actually is.

Small restaurants where the owner knows each staff member’s family situation, career ambition, and personal challenges retain staff measurably longer than those where staff are managed as interchangeable labour. This is not sentiment — it is the dominant retention mechanism available to you that a large chain cannot replicate.

You can’t compete with Qatar wages. You can compete on culture, recognition, and a future they can see.

02
Create a visible career path inside your kitchen

Kitchen staff in small restaurants often leave because they see no promotion path. They are a helper today and will still be a helper in 3 years.

Define explicit levels: Helper → Trainee Cook → Cook → Senior Cook → Sous Chef / Kitchen Lead. Each level has specific skills to demonstrate and a specific salary increase attached. Post it on the kitchen wall. Your best people will work toward the next level if they can see it clearly — and if they trust that you’ll honour the progression.

SLTDA-affiliated training schools and NAITA (National Apprentice & Industrial Training Authority) offer culinary and hospitality training programmes. Sponsoring a promising staff member to attend a short course costs relatively little and generates substantial loyalty — and a more skilled employee.

People don’t leave jobs they can see a future in. Build the ladder.

03
Comply with EPF/ETF — it is cheaper than you think in the long run

EPF/ETF compliance (employer EPF 12% + ETF 3% of wages) is legally mandatory. Many small operators avoid it to reduce costs. The risk: when a staff member leaves and files a complaint with the Department of Labour, the back-payments, penalties, and legal costs exceed years of contribution savings.

More importantly: staff who know their EPF is being paid are more willing to stay. It signals that you are a legitimate employer who values their future. In an industry where most small employers don’t pay EPF, the ones who do have a genuine recruiting advantage.

The national minimum wage is LKR 21,000/month (from March 2024, including BRA). Average hospitality wage is ~LKR 61,800/month. EPF/ETF on the minimum wage adds about LKR 3,150/month per employee. That is a small cost for legal compliance and improved retention.

Pay EPF. It is an investment in trust, not just a regulatory obligation.

04
Restructure shifts to reduce split-shift stress

Split shifts — where staff work morning prep, have a break, then return for dinner service — are the biggest quality-of-life complaint in Sri Lankan hospitality. A worker who commutes 45 minutes each way cannot go home during a 3-hour mid-day break. They wait at the restaurant, unpaid, and resent it.

Alternatives: (1) Convert to single long shifts where possible. (2) Pay staff for their break time if you can’t restructure the shift. (3) Hire breakfast-and-lunch staff and separate dinner staff, rather than expecting the same person to cover both.

This requires thinking about your menu and your business model. If you’re lunch-only, you don’t have a split-shift problem. If you’re full-day, redesigning shifts to be more humane is a direct retention investment.

The best way to retain staff is to make the job worth keeping.

05
Use slow months for training — not just cost-cutting

When business slows in August–September or post-Avurudu, most operators reduce staff hours or lay off temporary staff. The more effective operators use the quiet period to train, standardise, and promote from within.

Training your most promising staff member to take over kitchen management for one service per week gives you: a backup for when you’re absent, a staff member who feels valued, and a more skilled team at zero extra cost.

Facebook groups and Ikman.lk are the primary hospitality recruitment channels in Sri Lanka. The operators who fill vacancies fastest are those with a clear job description and a reputation as a fair employer. Your reputation as an employer is a hiring asset or liability — and it travels by word of mouth faster than any job post.

Train your best people during quiet months. They’ll repay you during the rush.

06
Calculate your actual cost of turnover — it’s higher than you think

Owners who focus only on monthly wages miss the full cost of staff turnover: recruitment time, training time (3–8 weeks for a new kitchen hire to reach competency), errors and waste during the learning period, and the productivity loss until the new person is fully effective.

A conservative estimate: replacing one kitchen staff member costs 6–10 weeks of that employee’s salary in lost productivity and training investment. If your monthly turnover costs you one replacement, that is a recurring hidden cost of Rs.40,000–100,000+ per year.

Once you know the true cost of turnover, the investment in retention — a small salary increase, EPF compliance, better shifts — often pays for itself many times over.

Retention is cheaper than replacement. Always. Do the calculation.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Diagnose your retention problem
Interview your most recent departed staff member (if possible): why did they leave?
Ask your 2 best current staff: what would make them leave? What keeps them here?
List your staff departures in the last 12 months: what was the pattern?
Calculate: what did each departure actually cost you (training, lost productivity, recruitment)?
3–4 hrs
week2
Build the structure
Define 3–4 career levels for kitchen and front of house. Write them down with skills and salary bands.
Post the career ladder in the kitchen. Discuss it with your best staff member individually.
Check your EPF/ETF compliance. If non-compliant, get a quote from an accountant on starting.
Audit your split-shift structure: can any shifts be converted to single long shifts?
4–6 hrs
week3
Invest in your best people
Identify your most promising staff member. Enrol them in one short training course (NAITA or SLTDA-affiliated).
Give them one new responsibility this month: menu input, ordering from one supplier, or training a new hire.
Acknowledge publicly (in front of the team) when staff do something well.
Have one individual conversation per month with each staff member about their work.
2–3 hrs/month
week4
Optimise your hiring when needed
Build a standard job description for each role. Post on Facebook groups and Ikman.lk.
Establish a 2-week paid trial period for new kitchen hires before permanent offer.
Build a referral bonus: Rs.2,000–5,000 to any staff member who refers a hire who stays 3+ months.
Track your average staff tenure. Set a target to improve it by 2 months this year.
2 hrs setup
After 4 weeks, you'll have:
✓
An exit interview done with your most recent departed staff member
✓
A 3-level career ladder posted in your kitchen
✓
EPF/ETF compliance status confirmed
✓
At least one shift restructured to reduce split-shift time
✓
Your best staff member enrolled in or booked for a training course
You can’t stop the migration. You can build a workplace that your best people prefer to stay in for one more year — and that one year is worth everything.

The hospitality industry in Sri Lanka is losing staff faster than it can replace them. The operators who thrive are the ones who build environments that make their best people choose to stay — not because they have to, but because it’s genuinely worth it.

Start this week: have a one-on-one conversation with your most reliable staff member. Ask them what they want from the next year. Then decide what you can offer.
