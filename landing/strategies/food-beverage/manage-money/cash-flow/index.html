“December was my best month in years. And yet by February I was struggling to pay rent. Where did it go?”
“I know I’m profitable. But there’s never actually cash in the account when I need it.”

Profit and cash flow are two different things. A profitable restaurant can run out of cash. Understanding where your money goes — and when — is the difference between surviving your slow months and not.

01
Map your cash flow timing — revenue and costs don’t always match

Sri Lankan F&B cash flow has a specific structure: Daily cash sales arrive today. Delivery platform payouts arrive weekly (PickMe Food, Uber Eats). Catering deposits arrive months before events; balance payments arrive after. Monthly fixed costs (rent, staff wages, utilities) arrive on fixed dates regardless of how your revenue is flowing.

The cash flow crisis most common in SL: a big December/Avurudu event generates high revenue, most of which is spent on supplies and extra staff immediately. January and February are slow. But rent, wages, and utilities don’t slow with them. The surplus from December is already gone when the slow months arrive.

Map it: for each month of the year, write your expected revenue and your expected fixed costs. Where the gap is negative, you need a plan. Where the gap is positive, you should be saving.

Cash flow is a timing problem. The money was there — it just left before the bills arrived.

02
Separate your operating buffer from your spending

The fix for most cash flow problems is the same: maintain a separate operating buffer account that covers 2–3 months of fixed costs, and treat it as untouchable except in genuine emergencies.

Build this buffer during your peak months by setting aside 15–20% of peak month net profit. A December that generates Rs.200,000 net profit should put Rs.30,000–40,000 into the buffer. That buffer funds February when foot traffic drops.

Wish Bakers (15 outlets) absorbed tripled operating costs during the 2022 crisis and maintained their prices by drawing on reserves. The buffer is not a savings account — it is an operating necessity for a seasonal F&B business in Sri Lanka.

The buffer exists for one reason: to pay your fixed costs during your worst months without disrupting your business.

03
Manage your delivery platform float carefully

Delivery platforms pay weekly, not daily. If you do Rs.50,000 of delivery orders on Monday, you won’t receive that money until Friday or the following Monday.

This creates a float — money you’ve earned but haven’t received. For restaurants doing high delivery volumes, this float can be Rs.100,000–300,000 at any time. If you spend today’s cash assuming that float is accessible, you’ll run out of cash before the settlement arrives.

Track your delivery float separately: running total of unreconciled orders. Don’t plan spending against that float until it arrives in your account. Platform settlements also have deductions — refunds, promotional costs — so the amount you receive is always slightly less than you expect.

Earned revenue and received revenue are two different numbers. Only plan spending against what’s in your account.

04
Pay yourself a fixed salary — not whatever is left

The most common cash flow destroyer in a family food business: the owner draws cash from the register whenever personal expenses arise. This makes the business’s cash balance unpredictable and often negative at the worst times.

Fix: decide your monthly salary. Transfer it from business to personal on the 1st of every month. Nothing else from the business account is personal spending. If the business can’t afford your salary one month, that is a signal — not a reason to draw more.

This discipline is hard at first and becomes automatic after 3 months. Once you have it, your business bank balance is a real number that you can plan against. Without it, the number is meaningless because it can drop to zero at any moment for personal reasons.

A fixed salary makes your business’s cash position real and plannable.

05
Build a 13-week cash flow forecast

A 13-week (3-month) rolling cash flow forecast is the tool every F&B business owner needs but almost none in Sri Lanka use. A Google Sheet with: week, expected cash in (sales + delivery + catering deposits), expected cash out (ingredients, wages, rent, utilities, commissions), closing balance.

Update it every Monday. It takes 15 minutes and tells you, 13 weeks in advance, which week you will run out of cash if current trends continue. That early warning gives you 13 weeks to act — not 13 days.

The December windfall that disappears by February is predictable. A 13-week forecast makes it visible in October so you can plan for it. The July supplier price spike is predictable. The cash forecast shows you it’s coming.

A 13-week view gives you time to act. A weekly view only gives you time to panic.

06
Collect catering deposits immediately — at least 50%

Catering is the highest-margin F&B category in Sri Lanka (net margin 15–30%). But catering has unique cash flow risks: you incur all costs upfront (ingredients, extra staff, equipment) and the client pays at or after the event.

Non-negotiable policy: minimum 50% deposit at booking confirmation. Balance due 48 hours before the event. This policy protects you from last-minute cancellations, client payment delays, and the cash flow gap between your event costs and event revenue.

Wedding season (November–March) generates the year’s largest catering bookings. Bookings are confirmed 12–18 months in advance. A 50% deposit on a Rs.500,000 wedding catering contract collected 12 months early is Rs.250,000 of working capital available for a full year before you spend a rupee on the event.

Catering deposits are working capital. Collect them at booking, not at delivery.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Map your cash flow
Draw your annual revenue pattern by month. Mark your 3 highest and 3 lowest months.
List every fixed monthly cost and the date it falls due.
Calculate your cash gap in your 2 lowest months: expected revenue minus fixed costs.
That gap is your minimum buffer requirement.
2–3 hrs
week2
Build your buffer
Open a dedicated buffer savings account (or sub-account).
During your next peak month: set aside 15–20% of net profit into the buffer.
Calculate: how many months to reach your 2-month fixed-cost target?
Set a monthly automatic transfer to the buffer account.
1–2 hrs
week3
Set up the 13-week forecast
Build a 13-week Google Sheet: week / cash in / cash out / closing balance.
Fill in the next 13 weeks with realistic estimates.
Identify: which week do you go negative? What can you do now to prevent it?
Update every Monday morning. 15 minutes maximum.
3–4 hrs setup + 15 min/week
week4
Tighten catering and platform policies
Update all catering quote templates: 50% deposit at booking, balance 48hrs before.
Track your delivery platform float in a separate cell in your cash tracker.
Transfer your fixed monthly salary to personal account on the 1st of this month.
Review: did any cash leave the business account for personal use this month?
2 hrs
After 4 weeks, you'll have:
✓
Your annual revenue calendar drawn with monthly high/low pattern
✓
A buffer savings account opened with at least the first month’s contribution
✓
A 13-week cash flow forecast running and updated weekly
✓
Catering deposit policy formalised at 50% on booking
✓
Your monthly salary defined and transferred consistently
The December–February cash cycle is the most predictable problem in Sri Lankan F&B. You’ve built the system to survive it.

Cash flow is not complicated. It is mostly the same problems, predictably timed, every year. Once you can see them coming — 13 weeks out — you have time to fix them. The forecast is the tool.

Start today: draw your annual revenue pattern by month. Mark your worst 3 months. Calculate your cash gap in those months. That number tells you exactly how large your buffer needs to be.
