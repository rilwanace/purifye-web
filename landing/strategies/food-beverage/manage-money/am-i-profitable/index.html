“The restaurant is busy every lunchtime. But I never seem to have any money at the end of the month. I don’t understand it.”
“I know I’m not losing money — I think. But I couldn’t tell you what my margin is.”

A busy restaurant that doesn’t know its profit margin is one unexpected cost away from a crisis. Most small Sri Lankan F&B operators have no P&L, no food cost tracking, and no clear view of whether the business is genuinely profitable or just cash-flow positive in good weeks. Here is how to find out — and what to do about what you find.

01
Calculate your gross profit — starting this week

Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold (COGS). COGS = all ingredient costs for everything sold. Target gross profit margin for most SL F&B businesses: 60–70% (meaning food cost = 30–40% of revenue).

This week: take your Monday–Friday revenue total. Take your ingredient purchases for the same period. Gross profit = revenue − ingredient purchases (adjusted for any opening/closing stock change). If your gross margin is below 55%, your food cost is too high relative to your prices.

Most small operators don’t track this. They know their cash in hand each evening but not their actual cost per unit sold. Gross profit is the foundation. Everything else — overheads, wages, rent — comes out of gross profit. If gross profit is insufficient, no amount of overhead cutting will save you.

Revenue minus ingredient cost. That one number tells you whether the business is fundamentally viable.

02
Build a one-page monthly P&L

A Profit and Loss statement doesn’t need to be complicated. One page, monthly, with these lines: Revenue (total), − Food/ingredient cost, = Gross Profit, − Staff wages, − Rent, − Utilities, − Delivery commissions, − Other costs, = Net Profit.

That’s 8 lines. Fill them in at the end of every month. After 3 months you have enough data to see your actual profitability trend, your seasonal pattern, and which cost line is eating your margin.

Most small operators in Sri Lanka track revenue in physical notebooks with no P&L. A Google Sheet template for this takes 30 minutes to set up and 20 minutes per month to fill in. It tells you more about your business than any other tool you could use.

One P&L per month. Eight lines. This is the financial heartbeat of your business.

03
Identify your profit per customer

Average revenue per customer × gross margin % = gross profit per customer. Minus your share of fixed costs per customer (rent + staff + utilities ÷ number of customers). = Net profit per customer.

For a restaurant serving 50 customers per day: if your daily fixed costs are Rs.25,000, you need Rs.500 of net contribution per customer just to break even. If your average order value is Rs.800 and your food cost is 35%, your gross contribution is Rs.520 per customer. That leaves Rs.20 net profit per customer before exceptional costs.

That number — Rs.20 net profit per customer — tells you exactly what’s at stake when you lose one table, miss one delivery order, or waste one portion. It also tells you that growing from 50 to 60 customers per day, or increasing your average order value by Rs.100, would have a dramatic effect on profitability.

Know your profit per customer. It makes every business decision concrete.

04
Separate your salary from your business profit

One of the most common illusions in small Sri Lankan F&B: the owner pays personal bills from the cash register and calls what’s left ‘profit.’ Or conversely: the owner doesn’t take any ‘salary’ and counts the business cash as personal income.

The correct approach: define a monthly salary for yourself (what would you pay someone else to do your job?). Deduct that as a business expense. Whatever is left after all expenses including your salary is the business’s net profit. If the business can’t pay you a salary and show a profit, the business model needs fixing.

This distinction is critical for tax planning, for understanding whether you should expand, and for knowing whether the business would survive without you personally running it 7 days a week.

A business that can only survive because you work for free is not a business. It’s a job that owns you.

05
Track your best and worst days — and understand why

Your best days teach you what conditions drive maximum revenue. Your worst days show you what kills it. Both are data you need.

Over 30 days: note each day’s revenue and 2–3 factors: weather, day of week, any special event, any operational issue. After a month you’ll see: do you always drop on Mondays? Do rainy days hurt foot traffic but boost delivery? Does the weekly Vesak Poya day affect you significantly?

This data drives your staffing decisions (fewer staff on your proven slow days), your purchasing decisions (smaller orders before consistently slow days), and your promotional decisions (run promotions specifically on your known slow days to lift them).

Data from your own business beats any industry benchmark. Collect it consistently.

06
Understand your break-even point

Break-even = total fixed monthly costs ÷ gross margin %. Example: Rs.400,000 fixed costs per month ÷ 65% gross margin = Rs.615,000 revenue needed to break even. Every rupee of revenue above Rs.615,000 is actual profit.

Once you know your break-even, you know: how many more customers per day would push you into profit, what price increase would lower your break-even point, and whether your current revenue trend makes the business sustainable.

Most small Sri Lankan F&B owners have never calculated their break-even. They’re making decisions about opening hours, new menu items, and expansion without knowing how many customers they need to cover their costs.

Know your break-even. Every decision after that gets cleaner.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Build the foundation
This week: calculate your gross profit for Monday–Friday (revenue minus ingredient purchases).
Set up a Google Sheet with the 8-line monthly P&L template.
Fill in last month from memory or receipts. It doesn’t need to be perfect.
Calculate your break-even: total monthly fixed costs ÷ gross margin %.
4–6 hrs
week2
Define your salary
Decide what you would pay someone else to do your job. That is your monthly salary.
Add it to your P&L as a staff cost.
Open a separate personal bank account. Transfer your salary to it on the 1st of every month.
What’s left in the business account after all costs including your salary is your profit.
1–2 hrs
week3
Track daily performance
Start your daily revenue + factor log tomorrow. 2 lines per day: revenue and key factors.
After 2 weeks: identify your best and worst days. What are the patterns?
Calculate your average profit per customer using this week’s data.
Identify the one change that would most improve your profit per customer.
5 min/day + 1 hr/month
week4
Monthly financial review
Fill in your monthly P&L on the 1st of each month for the previous month.
Compare to the previous month: is each cost line moving up, down, or flat?
Are you above or below break-even? By how much?
Set one financial target for the next month based on what you see.
1 hr/month
After 4 weeks, you'll have:
✓
Your gross profit margin calculated for this month
✓
A monthly P&L running for 3 consecutive months
✓
Your break-even point in rupees of revenue per month
✓
Your profit per customer calculated
✓
Your salary defined and separated from business profit
You now know whether you’re making money — actually, not approximately. That knowledge is the most valuable thing a small business owner can have.

A busy restaurant with no P&L is like a car with no fuel gauge. You might be fine. You might be about to stop. You have no way to tell until it’s too late.

Start today: take your last 5 days of revenue. Add up your ingredient purchases for the same period. Calculate your gross profit margin. That single number will tell you more than anything else.
