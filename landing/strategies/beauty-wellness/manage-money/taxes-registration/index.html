“I have been running this salon for three years and I still do not know if I am doing the tax thing correctly. I am scared someone will come and fine me.”
“My friend said I need to register for VAT. My accountant said I do not. I just want someone to tell me what a salon like mine actually needs to do.”
“I am operating informally because I never knew how to make it formal. But now I want to grow and I am not sure if being unregistered will cause problems.”

Most Sri Lankan salon owners either over-worry about compliance (fearing fines they are not actually exposed to) or under-worry (missing registrations that would protect them and open doors to credit). The reality is simpler than either extreme. Here is what a beauty salon in Sri Lanka actually needs — and when.

01
Start with the local authority business registration — it is your foundation

Every business operating in Sri Lanka, regardless of size or income, should have a local authority business registration from their Municipal Council, Urban Council, or Pradeshiya Sabha. This is the most basic registration and the one that legitimises your salon's right to operate at that address. It is also the registration most commonly requested by landlords, suppliers offering credit terms, and any institution you approach for a business loan.

To register: visit your local Municipal Council or Pradeshiya Sabha office and request a trade licence application. You will need your NIC, your lease agreement or proof of premises, and a completed application form. The fee ranges from Rs. 1,500–5,000 depending on your local authority and salon size. Renewal is annual, usually in January. If you are already operating without this, apply now — there is no penalty for registering late, only risk in continuing without it.

The local authority business registration is the first document every legitimate business in Sri Lanka should have.

02
Understand when income tax applies — and what your actual threshold is

Income tax in Sri Lanka applies to individuals with annual taxable income above Rs. 1,200,000 (as of the current Inland Revenue Act). For a sole proprietor salon owner, your taxable income is your salon revenue minus your allowable business expenses — not your total revenue. If Mihiri's salon earns Rs. 150,000/month (Rs. 1,800,000/year) but has Rs. 800,000 in allowable expenses (rent, staff salaries, products), her taxable income is Rs. 1,000,000 — below the threshold.

Register with the Inland Revenue Department (IRD) online at ird.gov.lk or at your nearest IRD office. You must register even if your taxable income is below the threshold — registration is separate from liability. Once registered, file your return annually by November 30. If your taxable income is below Rs. 1,200,000, you file a nil return. If above, you pay tax on the excess at 6% (up to Rs. 500,000 above threshold) and 12% beyond that. Keep all receipts for deductible expenses.

Most small salon owners pay zero income tax once expenses are correctly deducted — but you still need to register and file.

03
VAT: the threshold most small salons will not reach

Value Added Tax (VAT) in Sri Lanka currently applies to businesses with taxable supplies exceeding Rs. 80,000,000 per year (Rs. 80 million). A salon earning Rs. 3,000,000 per year is not required to register for VAT. A salon earning Rs. 80,000,000 per year is — and at that scale, you would have a professional accountant managing this. For the overwhelming majority of Sri Lankan salon owners, VAT is not a current requirement.

If a supplier or client tells you that you must register for VAT, confirm your actual annual revenue figure. If it is below Rs. 80,000,000, VAT registration is not required. If you voluntarily register for VAT (some businesses do to claim input tax credits on purchases), you must charge VAT to clients — a 18% addition to every service price. This is almost never beneficial for a small salon whose clients are price-sensitive individuals rather than VAT-registered businesses.

VAT is not relevant for most Sri Lankan salons. Focus on income tax and local registration first.

04
If you have staff: PAYE, EPF, and ETF obligations

Once you employ staff on a regular basis, three statutory obligations apply. PAYE (Pay As You Earn) tax: if any employee earns more than Rs. 100,000/month, you must deduct and remit tax on their behalf to the IRD. Most salon juniors in Sri Lanka earn Rs. 25,000–60,000/month, so PAYE typically does not apply. EPF (Employees' Provident Fund): you must contribute 12% of each employee's gross salary to EPF, and deduct 8% from their salary — total 20%. ETF (Employees' Trust Fund): you contribute 3% of each employee's gross salary. These apply to any employee with a regular working arrangement.

Register with the Labour Department and the Central Bank's EPF division. Monthly EPF and ETF payments are due by the last day of the following month. For a stylist on Rs. 35,000/month: EPF is Rs. 7,000 (Rs. 4,200 from you, Rs. 2,800 deducted from her), ETF is Rs. 1,050 from you. Total employer cost per month: Rs. 5,250 above salary. Factor this into your staffing costs from the start.

EPF and ETF are not optional if you have salaried staff. Underpayment carries penalties and creates legal exposure.

05
Keep the records that protect you — even informally

The IRD does not audit salons at random unless there is a specific trigger (a complaint, a large unexplained cash deposit, or a formal loan application that flags income inconsistency). The best protection is clean records — not necessarily formal accounting software, but at minimum: a monthly income log (total revenue per month, even from WhatsApp orders), a monthly expense log (rent receipts, product invoices, utility bills), and a separate record for staff salaries paid. These can be in a notebook or a simple spreadsheet.

For most sole-proprietor salon owners, a simple Google Sheet with monthly income and expense columns, plus a folder of receipts photographed on your phone, is sufficient to prepare an annual tax return. If your salon revenue is above Rs. 5,000,000/year, engage a registered professional accountant (not an informal bookkeeper) — the fee of Rs. 15,000–30,000/year for basic tax return preparation is worth the protection.

Clean records are not about compliance theatre. They are what protects you if anyone ever asks questions.

06
Registration opens doors that informal operation cannot

The practical reason to formalise — beyond compliance — is that registration opens access to formal credit, supplier credit terms, and government business support programmes. Sampath Bank, HNB, and BOC all offer SME loans to registered businesses with trading history. The Lak Sathosa and Divineguma programmes provide support to formally registered micro-businesses. NEDA (National Enterprise Development Authority) offers training and facilitation grants to registered SMEs. None of these are accessible to an unregistered business.

Once you have your local authority registration and IRD registration, open a dedicated business bank account (separate from personal). Even a basic savings account used exclusively for business income and expenses creates the transaction history that banks use to assess loan eligibility. Three years of consistent business bank statements — even if the amounts are modest — is more valuable than any document when approaching a bank for a growth loan.

Formalisation is not about compliance. It is about the doors it opens when you want to grow.

Your 4-week plan

Get properly registered and build the records that protect you.

week1
Get your local authority registration
Visit your Municipal Council or Pradeshiya Sabha office this week
Bring your NIC, lease agreement, and a completed application form
Pay the registration fee (Rs. 1,500–5,000) and collect your trade licence certificate
~half a day
week2
Register with the Inland Revenue Department
Go to ird.gov.lk or visit your nearest IRD office
Register as a sole proprietor (or company if applicable) and get your TIN number
Confirm whether your taxable income (revenue minus expenses) is above Rs. 1,200,000/year
~2 hours
week3
Set up your basic record-keeping system
Create a simple Google Sheet with columns: Month / Revenue / Expenses / Net
Photograph every receipt for the past 3 months and save to a phone folder labelled by month
If you have staff: confirm EPF and ETF registrations are in place
~2 hours
week4
Open a business bank account
Visit any major bank (Sampath, HNB, BOC, Commercial Bank) with your trade licence and TIN
Open a savings account in the business name used exclusively for business transactions
Start running all income and expenses through this account from this month forward
~half a day

Formalisation is not a burden. It is the foundation every growth decision stands on.

Most salon owners who avoid registration do so because they are afraid of what they will find. The reality is almost always better than the fear. The obligations for a small salon in Sri Lanka are modest — and the protection and access that registration provides are real.

This week: visit your local authority office and get the trade licence application form. Just getting the form is the first step.
