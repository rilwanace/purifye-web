“I have not raised my prices in two years. My costs have gone up but my prices have not. I know I should charge more but I am scared clients will leave.”
“I tried to raise my blowout price from Rs. 800 to Rs. 1,000 last year and one client complained so I never did it for anyone else. I have been stuck ever since.”
“The salon down the road charges 40% more than me. Their clients do not seem to mind. I do not understand why mine would be different.”

Sri Lankan salon owners consistently undercharge relative to the value they deliver — and consistently overestimate how many clients they will lose if they raise prices. The research on price increases in small businesses is clear: most clients accept modest price increases with no friction, provided the increase is communicated clearly and the quality they expect is maintained. The fear of losing clients prevents more income growth in Sri Lankan salons than any competitive factor.

01
Understand why you are undercharging — and what it is costing you

A salon owner who has not raised prices in two years has effectively given herself a pay cut. If product costs have risen by 20%, staff salaries by 15%, and rent by 10%, but prices have stayed flat, the net margin per service has fallen significantly. A blowout priced at Rs. 800 with 2022 product costs was earning a different margin than the same Rs. 800 blowout with 2024 product costs. The owner is working the same hours for less real income.

Calculate your effective hourly earnings. If a blowout takes 45 minutes and costs Rs. 800, your revenue rate is Rs. 1,067/hour before costs. After product cost (Rs. 150–200), staff cost (Rs. 250–300), and overhead allocation (Rs. 150–200), your net per blowout is Rs. 100–300. If you raised the blowout to Rs. 1,000, the net becomes Rs. 300–500 — a 50–100% improvement in net per service for a 25% price increase. This is the mathematics of why price increases matter more than volume increases.

Flat prices in a rising cost environment are a silent pay cut. The mathematics of a price increase are more powerful than adding new clients.

02
Know what the market actually charges — and where you sit in it

Before setting a new price, understand what comparable salons in your area charge. In Sri Lanka in 2025: a blowout at a basic neighbourhood salon is Rs. 600–900; at a mid-tier established salon Rs. 1,000–1,800; at a premium salon Rs. 2,000–4,000. A keratin treatment at basic: Rs. 4,000–8,000; mid-tier: Rs. 9,000–18,000; premium: Rs. 20,000–45,000. If your prices are at the bottom of the mid-tier range and your quality is mid-tier, you have room to move to the middle or top of that band without positioning yourself out of your market.

Visit or call 3–4 salons in your area and ask for their price list. Do this as a potential client, not as a competitor. Note their prices for your 5 most popular services. Calculate where you sit: bottom third, middle third, or top third of your local market. If you are in the bottom third and your quality is not at the bottom, you are underpriced. If you are already in the top third, a price increase requires a more deliberate quality or experience upgrade to justify it.

You cannot set the right price without knowing what the market actually charges. Research it first.

03
The 15–20% increase: small enough to keep clients, large enough to matter

The optimal price increase for a Sri Lankan salon that has not raised prices in 12–24 months is 15–20% across the board. This is the range that most price-sensitive clients accept without reducing visit frequency, while making a meaningful difference to the salon's net margin. A 10% increase often feels insufficient and still generates occasional pushback. A 30% increase can trigger significant churn among mid-tier clients. 15–20% is the evidence-based sweet spot for most Sri Lankan beauty businesses.

Apply the increase to all services simultaneously rather than selectively. Selective increases — raising some services but not others — create confusion and perceived unfairness. Give 4 weeks' notice to your regular clients. The notice itself signals transparency, which most clients respect. A blowout at Rs. 800 becomes Rs. 950. A gel manicure at Rs. 1,500 becomes Rs. 1,750. A keratin at Rs. 8,000 becomes Rs. 9,500. Round to convenient numbers — Rs. 950 not Rs. 960.

15–20% simultaneously across all services, with 4 weeks notice. That is the formula.

04
How to communicate a price increase so clients stay

The way you communicate a price increase matters as much as the amount. Clients who receive no notice and discover a higher price at checkout feel tricked — and some will leave not because of the price but because of the feeling of being ambushed. Clients who receive clear advance notice, with a simple explanation, almost always accept the increase and continue their relationship with the salon.

Send this message to your regular clients 3–4 weeks before the increase.

COPY-PASTE THIS
Hi [name], I wanted to let you know directly that from [date], Salon Mihiri will be updating our prices for the first time in [X] years. Our costs have increased — products, rent, and staff — and this adjustment allows us to keep our quality and our team. Our blowout will move from Rs. 800 to Rs. 950, and our full service menu will be updated on [date]. If you would like to book at current prices, we are happy to take appointments before then. Thank you for your continued trust in us. — Mihiri

Advance notice with a simple explanation keeps almost every loyal client.

05
The clients you will lose — and why it is usually a good thing

A 15–20% price increase will cause some clients to reduce visit frequency or leave. This is real and should be planned for, not denied. The clients most likely to leave: those who were booking primarily on price, bargain-hunters who use price as their main decision criterion, and clients who were already marginal — booking infrequently, negotiating on prices, and causing disproportionate friction. These clients typically represent 5–15% of your client base but a smaller percentage of your revenue — because loyal, quality-seeking clients book more frequently and spend more per visit.

For 15 days after a price increase, track your bookings daily and compare to the same period the previous month. Most salon owners in Sri Lanka who raise prices by 15–20% find that revenue in the first month after the increase is equal to or higher than the month before — because the revenue per client has risen while volume has only slightly declined. By month 2, most returning clients have rebaselined their expectations at the new price and the business has stabilised at a higher margin.

The clients who leave at a modest price increase were often your most difficult and least loyal. The ones who stay are your real business.

06
Premium service tiers: the path to higher prices without raising base prices

An alternative to across-the-board price increases: introducing a premium tier. A premium blowout at Rs. 1,800 that includes a hair mask treatment and a head massage sits alongside the standard blowout at Rs. 900. Some clients will choose the premium tier. You have effectively raised the average transaction value without telling any client that their regular service costs more. This approach works particularly well if your client base is price-mixed — some are price-sensitive, others are not.

Design one premium version of your 2–3 most popular services. The premium version adds: a treatment step (mask, serum, scalp massage), a longer time allocation, or an upgraded product (e.g. Moroccan oil instead of standard conditioner). Price it at 1.7–2x the standard service. When a client books, offer both options: 'We have the standard blowout at Rs. 900, and our premium blowout with an argan oil treatment at Rs. 1,500. Which would you prefer today?' Approximately 20–30% of clients will choose premium when offered clearly.

A premium tier raises your average transaction value without touching base prices. It is the gentlest price increase possible.
