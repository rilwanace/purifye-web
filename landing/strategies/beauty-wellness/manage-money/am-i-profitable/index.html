Strategies›Beauty & Wellness›Manage My Money Better›I have no idea if I'm actually making money
“End of the month, the bank account looks the same as last month. I genuinely don't know if Salon Mihiri made money or I just circled my own cash.”
“I'm busy every day. Clients are coming in. But I can't tell you if I made a profit last month. That should worry me more than it does.”
“I know my rent and my staff wages. After that it's a blur. I have no idea what I spend on products each month or what my actual take-home is.”

Cash-dominant businesses make profitability invisible. Most Sri Lankan salon owners cannot accurately report their monthly revenue, let alone their profit. Here is how to find the real number in one afternoon.

01
Calculate your monthly revenue — the actual number, not a feeling

Mihiri feels busy. Busy is not a profit number. The first step is recording every transaction for one month — not estimating, not averaging, actually recording. A notebook behind the reception desk. Niroshi writes the service and the amount for every client. At the end of the month, Mihiri adds it up. Most salon owners who do this for the first time are surprised — sometimes pleasantly, often not.

For Salon Mihiri: women's haircut Rs. 2,000, keratin Rs. 25,000, bridal package Rs. 65,000, gel manicure Rs. 5,500, facial Rs. 8,000, men's cut Rs. 1,500. On a day with 8 clients across these services, revenue could be Rs. 40,000–80,000. Multiply by 25 working days. The monthly number is probably larger than Mihiri thinks — and the profit is probably smaller.

Revenue is not profit. But you cannot calculate profit until you know revenue. Start there.

02
List every single cost — fixed and variable

Fixed costs (the same every month regardless of client volume): rent Rs. 95,000, Thanuja's salary Rs. 80,000, Kaveesha's salary Rs. 45,000, Niroshi's salary Rs. 35,000, electricity Rs. 15,000–25,000. Total fixed costs for Salon Mihiri: approximately Rs. 270,000–280,000/month before a single product is purchased.

Variable costs (change with client volume): professional colour, keratin products, skincare products, nail products, towel laundry, cleaning supplies. Estimated at 10–25% of revenue depending on service mix. On Rs. 500,000 monthly revenue, variable product costs run Rs. 50,000–125,000. Total costs: Rs. 320,000–405,000. Profit: Rs. 95,000–180,000. Profit margin: 19–36%. A well-managed mid-range Colombo salon should be at 20–35%.

Fixed costs do not care how busy you are. They are due whether you had 5 clients or 50.

03
Calculate profit by service — know which treatments actually make money

Not all services are equally profitable. Mihiri's keratin at Rs. 25,000 uses Rs. 2,200 in product (8.8% cost ratio) and takes 3 hours of a senior stylist's time. Her gel manicure at Rs. 5,500 uses Rs. 600 in product (10.9% cost ratio) and takes 60 minutes. The keratin earns Rs. 7,600/hour of stylist time. The manicure earns Rs. 4,900/hour. The keratin is more profitable per hour despite taking longer.

Calculate revenue per stylist hour for your top 5 services: (service price minus product cost) divided by service time in hours. This tells you which services to prioritise when you have a full appointment book, which services to promote, and which services might be underpriced. Many salon owners are surprised to find that their 'quick' services (threading, brow tinting) have very high hourly rates despite low absolute prices.

The service that feels quick and cheap might be your most profitable. Calculate before you assume.

04
Separate business and personal money — this week, not eventually

Mixing business and personal finances is the single biggest barrier to understanding salon profitability. If Mihiri pays the product order from her personal account, takes cash from the till for groceries, and pays her own salary irregularly, no bookkeeping system in the world can tell her if the salon is profitable. The money is one pool.

Open a business account if you do not have one — Commercial Bank, DFCC, People's Bank, any major bank. All salon revenue goes in. All salon expenses (rent, staff, products) come out. Mihiri's personal income is a fixed monthly transfer — her salary — not 'whatever is left'. Once accounts are separated, every financial question about the salon becomes answerable within minutes.

Once accounts are separated, profitability becomes obvious. Before that, it is always a guess.

05
Run a monthly P&L — 15 minutes on the first Sunday of every month

A profit and loss statement sounds complicated. For Salon Mihiri it is five numbers: (1) total revenue collected this month, (2) total product costs this month, (3) total fixed costs this month (rent + wages + electricity), (4) gross profit = revenue minus all costs, (5) profit margin % = gross profit divided by revenue times 100. Write these five numbers on one page, every month, on the first Sunday.

Month 1: revenue Rs. 480,000, products Rs. 85,000, fixed costs Rs. 275,000, profit Rs. 120,000, margin 25%. Month 2: revenue Rs. 410,000, products Rs. 72,000, fixed costs Rs. 275,000, profit Rs. 63,000, margin 15.4%. Month 2 is a problem. Without the monthly P&L, Mihiri would not know until Month 4 when the bank account is empty. With it, she knows in Week 1 of Month 2.

Teachers who do this monthly catch problems in 30 days. Salon owners who do not catch them in 12 months.

06
Understand the profit margins by tier — where should you be?

Estimated net profit margins for Sri Lankan salons by tier: budget neighbourhood salon Rs. 150,000–400,000/month revenue, net margin 15–30%. Mid-range Colombo salon (Salon Mihiri tier) Rs. 500,000–1,500,000/month revenue, net margin 20–35% if well managed. Premium salon (Noeline's tier) Rs. 2,000,000–5,000,000+/month revenue, net margin 25–40%. Mobile/home-visit beautician net margin 60–75% but capped by time.

If Salon Mihiri's margin is consistently below 20%, something specific is eating it — rent too high relative to revenue, product costs unmanaged, pricing too low on high-cost services, or staff costs out of proportion to revenue. The five-number monthly P&L reveals which one within three months of data.

A margin below 20% is not bad luck. It is a specific, findable, fixable cost problem.

Your 4-week plan

How to go from guessing to knowing your profit number.

week1
Record this month's revenue
Put a notebook at reception — Niroshi logs every service and amount for every client today onwards
Open a business bank account if you do not have one — any major bank, one afternoon
List every fixed cost: rent, each staff salary, electricity, any subscriptions
~2 hours
week2
Calculate your costs
Add up last month's product receipts from Nalaka and any other supplier — total variable cost
Add fixed costs to variable costs — this is your total monthly cost number
Calculate profit for last month: estimated revenue minus total costs
~1.5 hours
week3
Calculate per-service profitability
Pick your top 5 services and calculate: (price minus product cost) divided by hours = revenue per stylist hour
Identify your most profitable and least profitable services — the answer may surprise you
Flag any service where product cost is above 25% of the service price
~1.5 hours
week4
Run your first monthly P&L
Write the 5 numbers for this month: revenue, products, fixed costs, profit, margin %
Compare margin to the 20–35% benchmark for a mid-range Colombo salon
Set a recurring reminder: first Sunday of every month, 15 minutes, same 5 numbers
~30 minutes
After 4 weeks, you will have:
✓
Revenue recorded for every service — a real monthly number, not a feeling
✓
All costs itemised: fixed and variable
✓
Per-service profitability calculated for your top 5 treatments
✓
A business bank account with all revenue flowing through it
✓
First monthly P&L completed and a recurring habit set
You can now answer 'am I making money?' in under a minute.

Every salon owner who finally ran their numbers says the same thing: they expected it to be worse. Sometimes it is. Either way, knowing is always better than guessing — because you can only fix what you can see.

Write down your three biggest monthly costs right now. Rent, one staff salary, products. Add them up. That is your cost floor. Everything above that is profit. Start there.
