Strategies›Beauty & Wellness›Manage Appointments & Ops›I am doing everything myself and I am exhausted
“I open, I do the bookings, I serve the clients, I clean up, I order the stock, I handle the Instagram. I cannot keep going like this.”
“I have two staff but I still end up doing everything because it is easier than explaining it to them and then fixing their mistakes afterwards.”
“I have not had a proper day off in 8 months. I tell myself I will fix it next month. Next month never comes.”

The salon owner who does everything is the most common and most dangerous business model in Sri Lanka's beauty industry. Not because the owner is incompetent — usually the opposite. But a salon that runs on the owner's personal effort cannot grow, cannot survive the owner's illness or absence, and inevitably leads to burnout. The solution is not to work harder. It is to stop doing the things that someone else can do.

01
Diagnose what you are actually doing — and what is costing you most

Most salon owners who feel they are doing everything have not actually listed what 'everything' consists of. When Thanuja writes it down, it is usually: answering all WhatsApp booking messages (1–2 hours/day), managing the appointment book (30 mins/day), ordering products (1 hour/week), doing the weekly cash count and banking (1 hour/week), handling all client complaints (as needed), doing Instagram posts (2–3 hours/week), opening and closing the salon (30 mins each). That is 15–20 hours per week of tasks that are not actual beauty services. That is half a working week.

Write down every task you personally do in a week. Next to each one, write: (A) only I can do this, (B) someone could do this with training, (C) someone could do this right now with basic instruction. Tasks in category C should be delegated this week. Tasks in category B need a training decision. Tasks in category A — your actual technical skill, complex client relationships, business decisions — are what you should be spending your time on.

You cannot delegate what you have not listed. Write it down first.

02
The first thing to delegate: WhatsApp booking messages

Answering WhatsApp booking messages is the single most time-consuming non-revenue task most salon owners do — and it is almost always done by the owner because 'no one else knows the schedule.' This is the first task to transfer. The junior or receptionist does not need to know the whole schedule to answer 80% of booking messages. They need to know: what services you offer, which time slots are available today and tomorrow, how to say 'let me check and come back to you' for complex requests, and where to escalate.

Write a simple one-page cheat sheet: your service list, your prices, your available slots this week, and 4–5 template responses for common messages. Have Niroshi answer all WhatsApp messages for one week while you are in the salon. Correct her responses the same day in person — not by taking the phone back, but by saying 'next time when someone asks that, say this.' After two weeks she will handle 80% of messages correctly. You check the remaining 20%.

Delegating WhatsApp messages gives you back 1–2 hours every day. It is the highest-value first step.

03
Train your junior to handle opening, closing, and setup — not just service tasks

Most salon juniors in Sri Lanka are trained to assist with services but not to run operational tasks. This is a training gap, not a capability gap. A junior who knows how to set up stations correctly, check that supplies are stocked before the day starts, greet walk-in clients, and handle the salon closing routine is a different asset than one who only washes hair and hands towels.

Write a one-page opening checklist and a one-page closing checklist. Spend one week doing the opening and closing with your junior, checking off each item together. The second week, she does it while you watch. The third week, she does it independently — you check the result, not the process. This is not about perfection on day one. It is about building a routine that transfers. A junior who has run the salon opening correctly 15 times in a row does not need you there for the 16th.

A checklist transfers a task. Explaining it verbally every morning does not.

04
Stop being the only person who handles unhappy clients

When a client complains — about the result, about the wait, about the price — most salon owners step in immediately and personally resolve it. This is admirable but creates a dependency: every complaint escalates to the owner because that is the learned behaviour. Clients and staff both know that if they push, the owner will appear and fix it.

Give your senior stylist or receptionist the authority to handle specific complaint types without escalating. Define clear resolution boundaries: a client unhappy with a blowout result can be offered a redo within 3 days — Niroshi has authority to offer this. A client unhappy with colour needs the owner's assessment — escalate. A client unhappy with a wait is offered a future discount — Niroshi has authority. Write these down. When a complaint comes in, your staff handle it first. You review what they decided after the fact, not in the moment.

Authority without boundaries paralyses. Authority with clear limits empowers.

05
Your value is in decisions and relationships — not administration

The owner of Salon Mihiri is irreplaceable for: advanced technical services (keratin, colour correction, complex bridal), long-term client relationships with the salon's most loyal clients, business decisions (pricing, new services, hiring), and quality standards that define the salon's reputation. She is not irreplaceable for: answering WhatsApp, counting cash, ordering shampoo, or cleaning the basins. If the owner is spending 4 hours a day on tasks in the second category, the salon is misallocating its most valuable resource.

Every month, do a time audit — write down how you actually spent each day for one week. Mark each activity: revenue-generating skill / relationship-building / decision / administration / physical task. If more than 30% of your hours are in the last two categories, something you are doing should be delegated. This is not about doing less. It is about doing the things that only you can do.

Your time in the salon should be spent on the things only you can do. Everything else is a training task.

06
Build systems, not dependence on yourself

The goal is a salon that runs correctly when you are not there — not perfectly, but correctly. That means: written checklists for opening and closing, a service price list that any staff member can quote from, a stock ordering schedule that someone else can follow, a booking system that is not held in your head. None of these require expensive software. They require you to write down what you currently do in your head and give it to someone else.

Start with the single most repeated daily task you do that someone else could do. Write down exactly how you do it — every step. Give it to your senior junior to do for one week while you watch. Correct in real time. After two weeks, step back. Repeat with the next task. In three months you will have transferred 5–8 tasks. Your days will look different. The salon will look the same from the outside — which is the point.

A salon that runs without the owner present is worth building. A salon that cannot is not.

Every salon owner who feels trapped doing everything built that trap themselves — not through failure, but through being better than everyone else and forgetting to transfer the knowledge. The way out is to start writing down what you know and handing it over, one task at a time.
