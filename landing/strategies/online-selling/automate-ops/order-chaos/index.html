“WhatsApp was designed for conversations, not for order management. Using it as your fulfilment system is like using a notebook as your bank.”
“Every missed order is a visible failure. Every double-sold item is a buyer who will tell people. Every wrong packing is a return you pay for.”
“Order chaos does not get better with more orders. It gets worse exponentially.”

You take orders on WhatsApp, track them in a chat, pack based on what you remember, and occasionally realise mid-pack that you already sold that last unit to someone else three hours ago. At five orders per day this is manageable. At fifteen it is a crisis. At thirty it is a business-ending problem. The solution is not a complicated system — it is any system, consistently applied, that separates orders from chat and gives you a single view of what needs to happen next.

01
Stop using chat threads as your order tracking system

The WhatsApp chat thread is a communication tool. It records the conversation. It does not show you all pending orders at a glance, it does not flag what needs packing today, and it does not tell you when a buyer has not paid. Using it as your order management system requires you to hold all of that information in your head — a cognitive load that grows with every order and fails predictably.

The minimum viable replacement: a shared Google Sheet or a simple app where each row is one order. Columns: buyer name, contact, product, size/variant, quantity, price, payment status, dispatch status, courier tracking. Every order gets its own row. You view the sheet, not the chat, to know what to do next.

This is not a technology upgrade — it is a discipline shift. The first week feels slower because you are adding the extra step of logging each order. After two weeks it feels normal. After four weeks you cannot imagine going back to managing from chat because the improvement in visibility is so significant.

If you use Instagram or Facebook for orders too, route everything through the same sheet. Every channel's orders in one place means one view of your fulfilment state — not a different mental tab for WhatsApp, Instagram, and Daraz.

A list you can see is a list you can manage. A thread you have to scroll through is neither.

02
Standardise your order intake with a fixed format

When buyers order by WhatsApp message in free-form text — 'hey I want the blue one size 10 please' — you have to interpret, clarify, and re-confirm. Every order is a small negotiation. Multiply that by 20 orders a day and you have hours of back-and-forth that could be eliminated.

Create a fixed order form that buyers fill in — or that you fill in during the order conversation. Name, contact number, product code or name, size or variant, delivery address, payment method. Ask for all of these in one message, in order, every time. Most buyers appreciate the clarity.

For Instagram, a Google Form linked in your bio that pre-populates these fields eliminates the information-gathering phase entirely. Buyers submit the form, you receive a structured entry, it goes straight into your order log. This is a 30-minute setup that saves hours per week immediately.

Consistency in intake means consistency in packing. When every order row has the same fields in the same format, packing becomes a physical task rather than a detective exercise. You look at the row, you pull the product, you pack it, you update the status column.

A standard order format means you collect the right information every time — not whatever the buyer remembers to tell you.

03
Create a daily dispatch routine — same time, same process

Dispatching orders whenever they accumulate — some in the morning, some after lunch, some in the evening — means courier collection windows are missed, dispatch confirmations are sent at odd times, and your day is interrupted by packing every few hours.

Pick one or two fixed daily dispatch windows. 10am and 4pm, or just 11am. Everything received before the cutoff goes in that batch. Everything after the cutoff goes in the next day's batch. Communicate the cutoff to buyers in your standard order message: 'Orders confirmed before 9am dispatch same day. After 9am dispatch next morning.'

A daily routine converts the dispatch process into a defined block of time rather than a reactive interruption. You pack 15 orders in one hour at 10am rather than 3 orders at random intervals across the day. The same output takes less time and less mental energy when it is batched.

Within the dispatch window: print or display all that day's orders on your order sheet, pull stock in order, pack in order, write labels in order, update tracking numbers in order, send dispatch confirmations in order. Assembly-line thinking at a small scale.

A dispatch routine turns chaos into a scheduled event — one that you run, rather than one that runs you.

04
Track inventory in the same place as your orders

Most order chaos is actually inventory chaos. You sell something, the stock decreases, you forget to update, you sell it again, you have a problem. Inventory and orders are two views of the same system — tracking them separately doubles the chance of errors.

In your order sheet, add an inventory tab. Each product row: SKU or name, starting stock, sales (linked to orders), current stock. When an order is added to the order tab, deduct one from the inventory tab. This is a manual process at small scale but it takes 10 seconds per order and eliminates double-selling.

Set a reorder alert in the sheet — when inventory drops below X units, the row turns red or you add a manual note. This visual signal tells you to reorder before you run out, not after you have an unfillable order.

For sellers on multiple channels (Daraz + WhatsApp + Instagram), shared inventory is your biggest risk. If you have 5 units and sell 3 on Daraz and 3 on WhatsApp in the same hour, you have a problem. Allocating a fixed split — 3 units for Daraz, 3 for direct — is imperfect but prevents the worst case until you have a proper multi-channel system.

Inventory and orders are the same system. Track them together.

05
Handle payment status clearly — not by memory

A significant fraction of order chaos is payment chaos. Who has paid and who has not? Who promised to pay on delivery and then went silent? Which COD orders are still with the courier and which have been returned? These are operational facts that cannot live in your head across 20 or 30 orders.

In your order sheet: a payment status column with simple values — Paid (confirmed in account), Pending (COD or awaiting transfer), Returned (COD rejection). Every row has a status. Nothing dispatches without a Paid or COD status confirmed. Pending status orders are reviewed daily and followed up.

For buyers who promised to pay and have not: a single follow-up WhatsApp message 24 hours after order is reasonable business practice. 'Your order is ready to dispatch — please confirm payment transfer to hold your item.' Most genuine buyers respond immediately. Non-responders after 48 hours are unlikely to convert — free the inventory and move on.

Your COD settlement tracking also belongs here. For each COD order dispatched, note the courier and expected settlement date. When settlement arrives, update the row. This tells you your actual outstanding COD receivable at any point in time — an important cash flow input.

Payment status in your head is a liability. Payment status in a column is a fact you can act on.

06
Move to a proper order management app when WhatsApp volume exceeds 20 per day

A Google Sheet handles 5 to 20 orders per day well. Above 20, the manual data entry, the lack of automation, and the increasing complexity of multi-channel management starts to outweigh the simplicity benefit. This is the inflection point where a simple order management tool pays for itself immediately.

Options for Sri Lankan sellers at this scale: Shopify (if you have or want a website) includes an order management system that can accept WhatsApp and manual orders alongside website orders. Bitrix24 and Zoho are free-tier options with basic order tracking. For Daraz-heavy sellers, the Seller Centre itself handles Daraz orders — your system needs to handle only the non-Daraz volume.

The transition does not have to be perfect on day one. Run the new system parallel to your spreadsheet for one week. When you are confident the new system is capturing everything correctly, retire the spreadsheet. A messy transition is less expensive than staying in a system that cannot scale.

The right question is not 'what is the perfect system?' It is 'what is the simplest system that handles today's volume reliably, and what is the next system I move to when I outgrow it?' Solving for today's volume is always cheaper than over-engineering for a future that may not arrive on your expected timeline.

The best order system is the simplest one that does not lose orders at your current volume.

week2

Draft the information you need for every order in sequence

Test it on your next 5 orders and refine based on what information is still missing
Add it to your WhatsApp Quick Replies for one-tap sending
1 hr
week3
Establish your daily dispatch routine
Pick your dispatch window(s): one or two fixed times per day
Update your standard order message to include your cutoff time
Batch all pending orders for your first dispatch window tomorrow
Time the process: note how long it takes to pack and log 10 orders in one batch
1 hr setup, then daily
week4
Clear and categorise your current order backlog
Review all open WhatsApp conversations and identify unpacked orders
Enter every open order into the sheet with current status
Follow up on any orders awaiting payment for more than 24 hours
Dispatch everything that is paid and packaged in today's batch
2-3 hrs (one-time)

Order chaos does not get better with patience — it gets better with a system. The system does not need to be perfect. It needs to be consistent.

At 5 orders a day, WhatsApp tracking feels fine. At 20 it is a warning sign. At 30 it is a crisis. The time to build the system is before the crisis, not during it.

Today: create the Google Sheet. Enter your current open orders into it. Do not dispatch anything today without updating that sheet first. That is day one of your system.
