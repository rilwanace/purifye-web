“Every seller gets scammed at least once. The second time is your own fault — not because you were foolish, but because you had not built a system.”
“Fake payment screenshots are easier to create than you think and harder to detect than you think — suspicion alone is not a defence.”
“The best scam prevention is a process, not paranoia.”

You dispatched an order, the buyer sent a screenshot showing a successful transfer, and you thought the money was on its way. Three days later you check your account: nothing. By then the buyer has the product and is not responding. This happens to Sri Lankan online sellers every week — across WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, and even Daraz. The scams are not sophisticated. They rely on the gap between what a screenshot shows and what is actually in your account. Closing that gap is the entire solution.

01
Never dispatch based on a screenshot — only on what you see in your account

This is the single rule that prevents the majority of payment fraud. A screenshot shows what the buyer wants you to see. Your bank app, your bKash app, your PayHere dashboard — those show what actually happened.

The rule is simple and must be absolute: log in to your own payment account and confirm the specific transaction before you pick up a single item to pack. The amount must match. The timestamp must be plausible. The transaction must actually exist in your account — not in the screenshot.

Common objections: 'It takes too long to check every order.' It takes 30 seconds per order. 'The buyer will get impatient.' Any genuine buyer who sends a real payment will wait 10 minutes for your confirmation. Only a scammer will pressure you to dispatch immediately without verification.

Add this to your standard order message: 'Once I confirm receipt of payment I will dispatch your order — I will update you within 30 minutes.' This sets the expectation, normalises the process, and gives you a legitimate reason to verify before acting.

Verification is not distrust — it is the professional standard for every seller who wants to stay in business.

02
Know the three most common buyer scams in Sri Lanka

Fake bank transfer screenshots: The buyer sends a screenshot of a successful transfer that was either edited from a real transaction, generated using a fake banking app interface, or screenshotted from a real transaction to someone else. All three are indistinguishable by eye — only your bank app can confirm.

Delivery denial fraud: The buyer claims the parcel never arrived despite courier tracking showing delivered. They request a replacement or refund. This is particularly effective when the courier has no signature confirmation and the buyer is sophisticated enough to escalate to a Daraz dispute.

Chargeback fraud: Less common for COD but relevant for card payments via PayHere or online platforms. The buyer makes a genuine payment, receives the product, then disputes the transaction with their bank claiming they did not authorise it. The bank reverses the payment. This is rarer in Sri Lanka than in card-dominant markets but is increasing as digital payments grow.

The pattern across all three: the scammer targets sellers who have no verification system, who are too trusting of screenshots, or who do not document the transaction trail. Having a system is not just ethical — it creates the paper trail that protects you in disputes.

A scammer targets the path of least resistance — be the seller who removes that path.

03
Call COD buyers before dispatch for high-value orders

COD rejection costs you the outbound courier charge plus the return charge, plus the inventory cycle time. For low-value orders the cost is manageable. For orders above LKR 3,000 to 5,000, a COD rejection is a significant loss.

For high-value COD orders, a pre-dispatch verification call takes two minutes and dramatically reduces rejection rates. Confirm the name, the address, and that the buyer is still expecting the delivery. A scammer placing a fake order will often not answer or will give inconsistent details.

This call also catches genuine buyer mistakes: wrong address given at order time, buyer who has moved, buyer who ordered and forgot. Both are better resolved before dispatch than after. The buyer who placed a genuine order and gave the wrong address will be grateful you called. The buyer who was testing your system will have their test fail.

Set your threshold based on your margins. If a rejection of a LKR 2,000 order costs you LKR 560 in courier round-trip, the call is worth it even at that level. If your margins are thin, your threshold should be lower.

Two minutes before dispatch protects you from days of cost and frustration after it.

04
Verify digital payments in the app — not from the notification

Push notifications from payment apps can be spoofed on some Android configurations. A sophisticated scammer could trigger a fake notification on your phone while sending you a doctored screenshot. This is edge-case, but it is not hypothetical.

The defence is the same as the screenshot defence: open the app itself, check the transaction list, confirm the amount, confirm the sender name or number, confirm the time. Do not rely on the notification banner. Do not rely on the screenshot. Only rely on what you see inside the app.

For PayHere: log in to your PayHere merchant dashboard and check transactions there — not just the email or SMS notification. For LANKAQR: check your linked bank account or the LANKAQR merchant portal. For direct bank transfers: log in to your bank's mobile app and check the transaction history for the specific amount and date.

If a payment shows in your bank account but with a different name than the buyer gave you, do not ignore this. It is sometimes innocent (paying from a family member's account) and sometimes a red flag. Ask the buyer to explain before dispatching.

The app is the source of truth. Everything else is a representation of it.

05
Understand which payment methods have buyer dispute protection

Not all payment methods are equal when a dispute arises. Knowing the dispute mechanisms of each method helps you choose how to accept payment and how to respond when something goes wrong.

COD: no dispute mechanism — the transaction is physical and immediate. Daraz-mediated payments: Daraz has a buyer protection policy that sellers must understand. If a buyer opens a dispute on Daraz, you need delivery evidence and communication logs to counter it. Screenshot your dispatch confirmation and tracking number for every Daraz order.

PayHere: PayHere transactions are logged in their system and can be referenced in disputes. They have a merchant support process. Card chargebacks via PayHere follow standard card network rules — you need delivery proof and buyer communication logs to win a chargeback. Keep these records for at least 6 months.

LANKAQR: QR transactions through bank accounts are irrevocable once completed — the buyer cannot reverse a successful QR payment the way they can dispute a card charge. This is one structural advantage of QR over card for sellers who face chargeback risk.

Your choice of payment method is also a choice about your dispute exposure.

06
Build a blocked-buyer list and share it with your network

When you identify a scammer — a confirmed fake payment, a delivery denial fraud, or a serial returner — record their details: phone number, name used, address, platform username. This list serves you in two ways. It prevents re-engagement with the same person, and it becomes shareable intelligence.

Sri Lankan online seller communities on Facebook and Telegram regularly share scammer databases. When you identify a bad actor, post the details (phone number is usually the most useful identifier) in the communities where other sellers can see it. You have almost certainly been protected by information other sellers shared — returning that protection is both practical and the right thing to do.

For Daraz orders, use the Seller Centre to flag and block buyers where the platform allows it. Document the incident in your records: what was ordered, how the scam was executed, what communication you have saved. This documentation is useful if the same person escalates through Daraz's dispute resolution system.

The scammer who moves on to the next seller without consequence becomes a repeat offender. The ones you flag and share slow that cycle down. You are not just protecting yourself — you are protecting the ecosystem you operate in.

Information shared between sellers is the fastest scam-prevention network available.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Set up your payment verification rule
Write your standard message for payment-before-dispatch: 'I will confirm once I see the payment in my account — usually within 30 minutes'
Bookmark or pin your bank app, PayHere dashboard, or LANKAQR portal for fast access
Practise the verification flow for your most common payment method
Apply the rule to your next payment-based order without exception
1 hr
week2
Set your COD pre-dispatch call threshold
Decide your value threshold for pre-dispatch calls (suggest LKR 3,000-5,000)
Write a 3-sentence call script: confirm name, confirm address, confirm expecting delivery
Call on your next high-value COD order before dispatch
Note whether any details do not match — and what you do in that case
30 min setup, then per-order
week3
Join local seller community groups
Search Facebook for Sri Lanka online seller groups in your category
Join two or three active communities
Check if a shared scammer database exists in the group
Save the number format for posting scammer details when needed
1 hr
week4
Start your blocked-buyer log
Create a simple list: name, phone, platform, what they did
Add anyone from memory who was suspicious or confirmed fraud
Share with the seller communities if the incidents are confirmed
Check new COD orders against your list before dispatching
1 hr

A scam does not make you naive — it means you had not built the system yet. Build it now.

Scammers rely on speed — they want you to dispatch before you think. Your defence is a process that slows down that one decision. One check, every time, without exception.

Today: write your payment confirmation message and send it to yourself. Tomorrow: use it on every payment order. That single habit prevents the majority of payment fraud.
