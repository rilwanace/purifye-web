“Revenue in your bank account is not the same as profit in your business.”
“Most sellers discover they were working for less than minimum wage — after a year of believing otherwise.”
“Knowing your numbers is not accounting. It is survival.”

Money comes in, you buy more stock, money goes out, orders come in, you feel busy and assume the business is working. Then one month you look at your bank balance and it is lower than three months ago despite selling more. This is one of the most common and most dangerous positions for a Sri Lankan online seller to be in. You are not necessarily losing money — but you do not know. Not knowing is the same as losing, just slower.

01
Calculate your real unit economics for each product

Unit economics means: what does one unit of product actually cost to sell, and what does one unit actually earn? Most sellers know the selling price. Almost none of them know the true cost.

True cost of one unit: cost of goods (what you paid the supplier) + Daraz or platform commission (0 to 25% depending on category) + payment processing fee + packaging materials + courier cost + your COD rejection allocation + your time to pack and process.

The COD rejection allocation is where most sellers are surprised. If your COD rejection rate is 20% and your courier charges LKR 280 per return, then on every 10 orders you pay LKR 560 in return courier costs even when the product comes back unsold. That LKR 56 per order is real. It belongs in your cost calculation.

Do this calculation for your top three products first. Use real numbers from the past 30 days — not estimates. If the profit per unit is less than your time cost to process one order (even at a generous LKR 200 per hour), the product is losing money at scale.

The product that feels like your best seller may be your most expensive mistake.

02
Track your COD rejection cost as its own expense line

COD rejection is not just lost revenue — it is a direct cost. When a buyer refuses delivery, you pay the outbound courier charge (already spent) plus the return courier charge (typically 60 to 80% of outbound). You also get the product back, but it has been in transit, may be repackaged less attractively, and took a week out of your inventory cycle.

Sri Lankan industry rejection rates average 15 to 30% for unverified COD orders. If you are hitting 25%, one in four parcels you send out generates a courier cost with no revenue attached. That is not a bad luck problem — it is a structural cost.

Set up a simple rejection tracking sheet: date, product, courier, rejection reason if you can get it. After 4 weeks you will have a real rejection rate. Compare this rate across product types — some categories consistently reject more than others in Sri Lanka (high-value items, items with ambiguous photos, items where the buyer was price-shopping).

Once you know your actual rate, build it into your pricing. If your rejection rate is 20% and your round-trip courier cost per rejection is LKR 560, you are paying LKR 112 per order sent in expected rejection costs. That number belongs in your COGS calculation.

The courier return invoice you ignore every month is quietly destroying your margin.

03
Separate personal and business money today — not next month

This is the foundational step that most new sellers skip and every established seller eventually regrets skipping. When your business money and personal money are in the same account, you cannot know what the business is earning. You only know what the account holds.

Open a separate bank account for the business. Route all sales income to it. Pay all business expenses from it. Pay yourself a fixed monthly transfer to your personal account — that transfer is your salary, whether the number is LKR 15,000 or LKR 150,000.

You do not need a business registration to open a savings account designated for business use. Many Sri Lankan online sellers use a second personal savings account and simply enforce the discipline of separation themselves. The separation is what matters, not the account type.

The moment you separate accounts, your monthly profit becomes visible: business income minus business expenses equals business profit. That number was always there. Now you can see it.

You cannot manage what you cannot measure — and you cannot measure what is mixed.

04
Understand the difference between profit and cash

A business can be profitable and still run out of cash. This sounds contradictory but it is one of the most common financial traps in online selling. You made a profit last month — but that profit is currently sitting in 80 units of stock you have not sold yet, 12 COD orders the courier has not settled yet, and a Daraz payout that comes in 14 days.

Profit is what you earned. Cash is what you have. Both numbers matter, but only cash pays your expenses this week.

In Sri Lanka's online selling environment, the cash gap is structural. Daraz settles payments 7 to 14 days after delivery. COD couriers hold your cash for varying periods depending on their settlement terms. Stock you bought this week ties up cash until it sells and the courier pays you — sometimes 3 to 4 weeks later.

Track both numbers: your profit (what you earned on paper this month) and your available cash (what is actually in your business account right now). If profit is growing but cash is shrinking, you are likely growing faster than your cash cycle can support — a specific and solvable problem.

Profit is a story about the past. Cash is the reality of right now.

05
Know your break-even units every month

Break-even is the number of units you need to sell to cover all your fixed costs before you earn a single rupee of profit. It is the most important number in your business and most sellers cannot name it.

Fixed costs for a typical Sri Lankan online seller: mobile data plan, platform subscription (if any), packaging supplies baseline, and any rent you pay for storage. Variable costs are per-order: COGS, commission, courier, packaging per item, COD rejection allocation. Your break-even in units is your total fixed monthly cost divided by your contribution margin per unit (selling price minus variable cost per unit).

If your fixed monthly costs are LKR 8,000 and your contribution margin per unit is LKR 400, you need to sell 20 units to break even. Every unit above 20 is profit. Every unit below 20 is a loss for that month.

This number changes your decisions. When a slow month is approaching, you know how many units you need to recover. When a good month happens, you know how many units went toward profit versus covering costs. It converts vague anxiety about money into a specific, actionable target.

A seller who knows their break-even is never surprised. They are watching the right number.

06
Run a simple weekly P&L — it takes 15 minutes

A Profit and Loss statement sounds formal. In practice, for an online seller, it is a three-section list: revenue this week, cost of goods sold this week, operating expenses this week. Revenue minus COGS minus expenses equals your operating profit for the week.

Fill it in every Friday or Saturday before you close out the week. It takes 15 minutes when you have a template. It gives you a week-by-week view of whether the business is improving or deteriorating — which a monthly view often hides until the damage is significant.

What to include in weekly revenue: all payments received (Daraz settlements, direct payments, COD courier payouts). What to include in COGS: supplier invoices for goods sold this week (not goods purchased — goods sold). What to include in expenses: packaging, courier charges for outbound, any ad spend, platform fees.

After 4 weeks, you will have a baseline. After 8 weeks, you will be able to see trends. After 12 weeks, you will have more financial clarity about your business than 90% of Sri Lankan online sellers operating at the same scale.

Fifteen minutes of tracking every week prevents months of confusion.

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Calculate unit economics for your top 3 products
List: selling price, COGS, platform commission, packaging cost, courier cost
Add a COD rejection allocation based on your estimated rejection rate
Calculate gross profit per unit for each product
Identify which product is most and least profitable
3 hrs
week2
Open a dedicated business account and separate your money
Open a second savings account if you do not have one
Transfer a starting business balance — separate from personal funds
Route your next Daraz payout or COD settlement to this account
Pay yourself a fixed weekly or monthly amount to personal account
2 hrs
week3
Set up your weekly P&L template
Create a simple spreadsheet: revenue, COGS, expenses, profit
Fill in last week's numbers from memory and bank records
Identify the two numbers you were missing (probably COGS and courier)
Calculate your break-even units for this month
2 hrs
week4
Track one full week properly
Record every order, every payment, every courier cost as it happens
At end of week, complete your P&L for the first time with real data
Compare your profit number with what you thought you were making
Decide which products to prioritise based on real margin, not revenue
30 min/day

You will discover things that surprise you. That is the point. Knowing is always better than guessing.

Feeling busy is not the same as being profitable. Selling more is not the same as earning more. The sellers who build sustainable businesses are the ones who know the difference — and check regularly.

This week: do the unit economics calculation for your top product. Use real numbers from last month. Whatever you find, it is better to know now than discover it a year from now.
