You started selling to make money — not to become an accountant. So the registration and tax questions have stayed in the background, handled by ignoring them and hoping nothing happens. That strategy works until it does not. In Sri Lanka, the legal requirements for online sellers are straightforward once you understand them — and most sellers are either over-complicating the compliance or genuinely exposed to risk they are not aware of. This guide covers the practical reality, not the textbook version.

01
Understand your registration options: sole proprietor vs PVT Ltd

A sole proprietorship is the simplest structure. You and the business are legally the same entity. Registration is done at your Divisional Secretariat. The cost is minimal — typically LKR 500 to LKR 2,000 depending on the DS office and business type. You get a business registration certificate that you can use to open a business bank account, apply for payment gateways, and establish yourself as a legitimate business.

A Private Limited Company (PVT Ltd) is a separate legal entity. It offers limited liability protection — if the business is sued or has debts, your personal assets are protected. It is registered at the Registrar of Companies and involves more paperwork, ongoing annual returns, and audit requirements once you exceed a certain turnover.

For most Sri Lankan online sellers doing under LKR 5 million annually, a sole proprietorship is the right structure. It is simpler, cheaper to maintain, and has no mandatory audit requirement at that scale. PVT Ltd makes sense when you have partners you need legal clarity with, when you are taking on investment, or when your personal asset exposure becomes a real concern.

Do not let the fear of complexity stop you from registering. A sole proprietor registration at the DS office is a half-day task that most people overcomplicate in their heads.

The right structure is the simplest one that covers your actual situation — not the most impressive-sounding one.

02
Know the VAT threshold and when you actually cross it

In Sri Lanka, businesses with taxable turnover exceeding LKR 60 million per quarter (as of recent legislation — verify the current threshold, as this has changed) are required to register for VAT. For most small and medium online sellers, this threshold is well above their annual revenue, let alone quarterly.

If you are making LKR 500,000 per month in sales, your annual turnover is LKR 6 million. You are nowhere near the VAT threshold. The anxiety many sellers have about VAT is misplaced — it applies to a scale most independent online sellers are not approaching.

SVT (Simplified VAT, now replaced by the simplified income tax regime in many cases) and SVAT (Suspended VAT for manufacturers and exporters) are different things again. Most online sellers selling domestically are not involved in either. If you are exporting goods and have a relationship with a larger corporate buyer, SVAT may become relevant — that is when you consult an accountant.

The practical check: go to the Inland Revenue Department website or call the IRD helpline and ask directly. Tell them your current annual turnover. They will tell you whether you need to register for VAT. This is a ten-minute call that replaces months of anxiety.

The tax conversation is simpler than you fear — once you actually have it with the right people.

03
Register at the Divisional Secretariat — what it costs and takes

The business registration process at the Divisional Secretariat is often described as bureaucratically complex. In practice, for a sole proprietorship selling goods, the requirements are: your NIC, a filled application form, a small fee, and in some DS offices a sketch of your business location or a utility bill.

Processing time varies from same-day to two weeks depending on the DS office. Urban DS offices (Colombo, Gampaha, Kandy) tend to be faster simply from higher volume and experience with these applications. Your registration certificate is issued for one year and needs annual renewal.

Why bother if you are selling fine without it? Three practical reasons. First, many payment gateways and bank business accounts require it. Second, if you ever face a buyer dispute or courier claim that escalates legally, being an unregistered business weakens your position. Third, as platforms like Daraz tighten their seller verification requirements, a registration certificate may become a requirement to maintain your account.

The registration is not a declaration that you are running a large business. It is a declaration that you are running a legitimate one.

Registration is not a bureaucratic hurdle — it is the minimum legitimate foundation for a sustainable business.

04
Open a proper business bank account and use it

With your business registration certificate, you can open a dedicated business account at most Sri Lankan commercial banks. This is distinct from the informal second savings account approach — a formal business account gives you access to business loans, better payment gateway terms, and a clear paper trail for any tax assessment.

For most sellers, a savings account registered in the business name at Sampath, Commercial, or BOC is sufficient. Some banks have small business account products with no monthly fee for accounts with regular activity. Call ahead and ask what documentation they require — it varies by bank.

A business bank account also makes your tax position cleaner. The IRD increasingly looks at bank statements when assessing income. A business account with business transactions is far easier to explain than a personal account with a mixture of sales revenue, personal transfers, and household expenses.

If you are already using a personal account for business, do not panic. Move forward from today with a clean separation. Prior mixing is common and does not automatically create a problem — but continuing the mixing after you are aware of the risk does.

A business bank account is not bureaucracy. It is evidence that you have a real operation.

05
Understand what income tax you actually owe

Income tax for individual businesses in Sri Lanka is assessed on the IRD's personal income tax scale for sole proprietors. You pay on your net business income — revenue minus legitimate business expenses. Legitimate expenses include: cost of goods sold, courier costs, platform fees, packaging, phone and data used for business, and any rent for business space.

The first LKR 1,200,000 of annual income (as of recent tax year — verify current threshold) may be exempt or taxed at a low rate under personal relief provisions. Above that, rates escalate progressively. If your net business income is under this threshold, your tax liability is minimal or zero.

Sellers often assume online income is invisible to the IRD. This assumption is increasingly unreliable. Daraz and other platforms are required to report high-volume seller transactions. Bank transfers above certain amounts trigger reporting requirements. The informal economy assumption is becoming riskier as financial digitalisation increases.

The responsible approach: keep your expense records (supplier invoices, courier receipts, platform statements), file an income tax return annually even if your liability is zero, and pay what is actually owed. This takes 2 to 4 hours per year with good records — less than the time you spend worrying about it.

Taxes are not the enemy. Uncertainty is. Knowing what you owe converts a fear into a line item.

06
When to hire an accountant — and when it is a waste of money

A freelance accountant in Sri Lanka costs approximately LKR 5,000 to 15,000 per month for a basic monthly bookkeeping and quarterly tax filing service. For a seller doing under LKR 3 million annually with simple, trackable transactions, this cost is hard to justify — you can manage it yourself with a basic spreadsheet and a once-a-year tax consultant session.

The inflection point where an accountant is worth it: when your monthly revenue exceeds LKR 500,000 regularly, when you have employees, when you are dealing with multiple platforms and product categories, or when your business structure gets more complex (wholesale relationships, own website with multiple payment methods, inventory in multiple locations).

A better option for smaller sellers: an annual or quarterly session with an accountant who can review your records, confirm your tax position, and flag anything you have missed. This costs LKR 3,000 to 8,000 per session and gives you professional clarity without a monthly retainer you do not need.

If you do hire an accountant, interview them specifically on e-commerce and online business experience. General bookkeepers may not be familiar with Daraz settlement statements, COD courier invoicing, or digital payment gateway reconciliation. Ask directly: have you worked with online sellers before?

An accountant is leverage, not insurance. Hire them when the leverage value exceeds the cost.

Set up the account and transfer your business operating balance

week4

Gather last year's expense records: COGS invoices, courier receipts, platform statements
Calculate approximate net income for last year

4 hrs

Legal clarity is not glamorous. But it removes a weight you have been carrying without knowing it.

The sellers who avoid registration and tax compliance are not avoiding a burden — they are accumulating one. Every year it gets slightly harder to clean up and slightly riskier to continue ignoring.

This week: call the IRD helpline and your local DS office. Two phone calls. They will tell you exactly what you need to do. That is all it takes to turn this from a source of anxiety into a checked box.
