“I have 45 students paying me every month. I feel like I should be comfortable. But I never seem to have much saved.”
“I started a new A/L class this year — 12 students. I honestly don't know if it's making me money or costing me.”
“My friend has fewer students than me but seems more financially settled. I must be doing something wrong.”

Independent tuition can be one of the most profitable professions in Sri Lanka — but only if you know your numbers. Most teachers have good income and poor financial clarity. Here's how to close that gap.

01
Calculate your gross revenue — most teachers haven't done this

Students × monthly fee = gross revenue. If you have 45 students at LKR 3,000/month, that's LKR 135,000/month. If you have 45 students across 3 different fee levels, add them individually.

This takes 10 minutes. It's the foundation of every financial decision that follows.

Write down every student's fee and add them up. That number — the real number — is probably different from what you had in your head.

This takes 10 minutes. It's the foundation of every financial decision that follows.

02
Track your actual expenses against that revenue

Hall rent, printing, transport, phone, any staff costs. Subtract these from your gross revenue. That's your actual net income.

If your margin is significantly lower than 85%, something specific is eating it — and the expense tracking exercise will find it.

Independent teachers with one hall typically have 85%+ profit margins — because the primary input is your own time, not raw materials or staff.

03
Calculate break-even before starting any new class

That new A/L class with 12 students at LKR 4,000 each: LKR 48,000 revenue. Hall rented 3 sessions per week at LKR 1,000/session: LKR 12,000/month in venue cost. Net: LKR 36,000. Is that worth your time?

Every teacher who has ever expanded into an unprofitable class and closed it after 3 months skipped this calculation. It takes 5 minutes.

Before committing to any new class, new subject, or new hall: minimum students needed to cover costs = total monthly cost of that class ÷ fee per student. That is your break-even number.

Every teacher who ever closed an unprofitable class after 3 months did this calculation afterward. Do it before.

04
Calculate your actual hourly rate — it is usually lower than you think

Total monthly income divided by total hours worked — including admin, marking, parent messages, and travel. Not just teaching hours. If you earn LKR 120,000 and work 60 hours per week, your hourly rate is LKR 500. That number changes how you think about your time.

Most teachers who do this calculation find the number shockingly low. That is not a problem — it is information. It motivates either a price increase, an admin reduction, or both.

Monthly income ÷ total hours worked this month = your real hourly rate. Write it down.

The number is usually lower than expected. That's not a problem — it's the information that changes the decision.

05
The 5-minute monthly profit check

On the last Sunday of every month: total fees collected, subtract total expenses, record net income. Write it down. Compare to last month.

Teachers who do this monthly catch problems in 30 days. Teachers who don't catch them in 12 months — or when a bank statement reveals something alarming.

You don't need accounting software. You need a notebook and 5 minutes.

Teachers who do this monthly catch problems in 30 days. Teachers who don't catch them in 12 months.

06
Compare month-over-month — spot the patterns

January vs. April vs. August: your income pattern follows the exam cycle. Once you see it written down, you can plan around it.

Two years of monthly records gives you the information to make financial decisions confidently.

A teacher who knows January will be their best month saves differently than one who's surprised every April when cash runs short.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Run the numbers
Complete the income record — Every student, fee, class — and whether they actually paid last month
List every cost — Rent, printing, phone, internet, subscriptions — everything for teaching
Calculate profit and margin — Income − costs = profit. Profit ÷ income × 100 = margin. Write both down
~1.5 hours
week2
Find the real margin
Identify most and least profitable classes — Small class + high rent = worst margin. Large class + low overhead = best
Compare to the 85% benchmark — Above 85% = healthy. Below 70% = specific cost to address
Check every student against their actual fee — Any paying below your current rate? When did you last increase prices?
~1 hour
week3
Fix what's losing
Identify any class below minimum viable size — Under 6 students in a rented space may be losing money — run the numbers
Set a price increase date if needed — Rates unchanged for 2 years? Pick a real date — not "eventually"
Cut one cost that doesn't directly serve students — Any subscription or tool you could remove without affecting teaching
~1 hour
week4
Set the review habit
Block 15 min on the first Sunday of every month — The only financial habit that matters — everything else is one-time
Log next month's income forecast — Expected students × average fee — compare actual vs forecast at month end
Set a profit margin alert threshold — "If margin drops below 75%, something needs to change." Write it down
~30 min
After 4 weeks, you'll have:
✓
Gross revenue calculated every student × their fee — the real number
✓
Actual profit margin calculated revenue minus costs, expressed as a percentage
✓
Break-even checked for every active class and for any planned new ones
✓
Hourly rate calculated total monthly income ÷ total hours including admin
✓
Monthly profit check habit started 5 minutes, last Sunday of each month
You can answer 'am I making money?' in under a minute.

Teachers who ask "am I making enough?" usually already know the answer feels wrong — but they don't have the numbers to confirm it or fix it. The margin calculation you just read about is genuinely simple: one subtraction, one division. The teachers who've done it and found a problem were relieved, not surprised. Knowing the number is always better than guessing.
