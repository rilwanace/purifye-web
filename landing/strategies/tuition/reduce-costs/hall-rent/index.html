“I pay LKR 30,000 rent for a hall I use maybe 15 hours a week. The rest of the time it just sits there.”
“After rent and printing, I barely feel like I'm earning what my time is worth.”
“A teacher I know splits a hall with someone who teaches a different subject. I keep thinking I should do something like that.”

Hall rent is the most visible cost — but it's usually not the actual problem. Before moving or downsizing, check whether you're using what you're paying for.

01
Do the revenue-per-seat analysis before changing anything

Hall rent is visible. Half-filled seats are silent. If you're paying LKR 30,000 for a 50-seat hall but running only 25 students, your rent problem is actually an occupancy problem.

The math: 25 students at LKR 3,000 = LKR 75,000. Hall rent LKR 30,000. Net LKR 45,000. 40 students: LKR 120,000 revenue, same LKR 30,000 rent. Net LKR 90,000. The hall didn't change — the occupancy did.

Fill the hall before moving to a smaller one. Each additional student makes the same rent cheaper per head.

Half-filled seats are expensive. Full seats make rent invisible.

02
Stop renting empty hours

If you're renting a hall 6 days a week but only teaching 4, you're paying for 2 days of nothing.

Alternatively, rent the hall by the session rather than monthly. More expensive per session, cheaper if you're using fewer than 4 days a week.

Most hall rental agreements can be renegotiated — or you can switch to a 4-day arrangement. The worst they say is no. Most landlords prefer a reliable 4-day tenant to an empty hall.

You're paying for sessions that generate revenue. Empty hours have no return.

03
Share a hall with a different-subject teacher

A mathematics teacher and a chemistry teacher who both teach A/L students don't compete — they complement each other. Their students are the same parents, different subjects.

Find one compatible teacher in your area with a compatible schedule. One conversation, one arrangement.

Split the monthly rent. LKR 30,000 becomes LKR 15,000. That's LKR 15,000 permanently removed from your cost base, with zero change to how you teach.

One conversation, one arrangement, and your monthly rent becomes half.

04
Know when a hall makes sense (and when it doesn't)

A home-based teacher with a waiting list of 10+ students and a current batch of 25+ is ready for a hall. A home-based teacher with 15 students and no waiting list is not.

Do the calculation for your own numbers before signing any agreement.

At LKR 3,000/student, you need 10 new students to cover a LKR 30,000/month hall. If you can't generate 10 new students in the first month of having the hall, you're subsidizing the landlord.

A waiting list of 10 means the hall pays for itself in the first month. No waiting list means you're subsidizing rent.

05
A hybrid model permanently reduces your physical space needs

10 students attending physically + 20 students attending online = the same teaching effort and similar revenue, but a smaller required venue.

The hybrid model may be the most effective long-term tool for managing venue costs — as online adoption continues to grow.

A smaller room (cheaper rent) serves the physical students. Online students pay slightly less but have no venue cost for you.

As online revenue grows, physical space requirements shrink. The hybrid model is the most effective long-term tool for managing venue costs.

06
Calculate your break-even enrollment before committing

Before signing any hall agreement: monthly rent ÷ monthly fee per student = number of students needed just to cover rent. That's your break-even number.

One hour of calculation prevents 6 months of regret.

Can you realistically recruit that many new students in the first month? If yes, the hall makes sense. If not, the hall eats into your existing income.

One hour of calculation prevents 6 months of regret.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Map the usage
Log every hour the hall is in use this week — Class, prep, other usage — calculate how many hours sit empty
Calculate cost per student per hour — Monthly rent ÷ students ÷ hours — this frames every hall conversation
Check your rental agreement — Renewal date, notice period, earliest you could change the arrangement
~1.5 hours
week2
Explore alternatives
Approach the landlord about a usage-based rate — "I use 15 hrs/week — would you consider a per-hour rate instead?"
Ask 2 other teachers about space sharing — Split rent with someone using the hall at different times — most are open
Research one alternative venue nearby — School hall, community centre, temple — what does comparable space cost?
~1.5 hours
week3
Run the numbers
Calculate the home-based saving — Monthly saving factoring in disruption, perception, space limits
Calculate the subletting income — Empty hours × rate × occupancy estimate — realistic monthly figure
Compare all three scenarios on one page — Current vs renegotiated vs subletting vs move — numbers side by side
~1 hour
week4
Make the decision
Decide: renegotiate, sublet, move, or stay — Any of these beats doing nothing — commit to one path
Set the action step for this week — Renegotiation meeting, sublet post, venue visit, or a 3-month review date
Calculate what a LKR 5,000/month saving buys you — 5 months = one new student's fees — frame saving in teaching terms
~1 hour
After 4 weeks, you'll have:
✓
Revenue-per-seat calculated current fill rate, current net after rent
✓
Empty teaching slots eliminated or renegotiated or confirmed that none exist
✓
A hall-sharing conversation attempted or a compatible teacher identified
✓
Break-even enrollment calculated for current agreement the number you need to cover rent
You know whether your hall is working for you or against you.

Rent is the largest fixed cost for most teachers, and most teachers negotiate it exactly once — when they first sign the lease. The market has changed since then. Spaces sit empty. Landlords are flexible in ways they weren't two years ago. The teachers who've reduced their rent costs aren't the ones who got lucky. They're the ones who asked.

Find out what the empty space next door or around the corner is going for. One inquiry. You don't have to negotiate anything yet — just get the number.
