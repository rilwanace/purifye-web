“I have students outside my area who want to join my class. But I don't know how to do it properly.”
“I spent an hour trying to get Zoom working before my first online session. Three students couldn't connect and I gave up.”
“I don't know what to charge for online classes. The same as physical? Less? I have no idea.”

Online teaching is simpler than the setup anxiety makes it seem. Once the first session runs without problems, it becomes routine quickly. Here's the setup that works first time.

01
Zoom is the tool — here's the setup that works first time

Free Zoom allows 40-minute sessions (enough for practice, not full classes). Zoom Pro at LKR 1,500–2,000/month removes the time limit. Install on your tablet or laptop — not just your phone.

Settings to configure once: set a meeting password, enable waiting room, turn off participant screen sharing, mute participants on entry.

Do a test session with one trusted parent before your first real online class. 15 minutes. Check audio, screen sharing, and reconnection after a drop. This prevents the 3-student-connectivity disaster on launch day.

One bad first session loses the student before teaching starts. One good test session prevents it.

02
Price online classes at 60–70% of your physical rate

Online students don't get the in-person attention, the physical classroom energy, or the ability to walk up to you after class with their book. The price should reflect that.

That's still meaningful revenue for zero hall costs and zero travel time. And it lets you reach students outside your area who couldn't attend physically.

At 60–70% of your physical rate, the difference is fair and transparent. A physical class at LKR 3,000/month becomes LKR 2,000–2,100 online.

03
Record sessions as catch-up content

With student and parent consent, record your Zoom sessions. Students who miss a class can watch it back — they don't fall behind, they don't drop.

This isn't free labor. It's a retention tool that costs zero extra time.

Recording solves the single biggest disadvantage of online tuition compared to physical. A student who misses a physical class is behind. A student who misses a recorded online class watches it back over the weekend.

This isn't free labor. It's a retention tool that costs zero extra time.

04
Start a YouTube channel for long-term lead generation

10-minute subject explainers in Sinhala or Tamil — one per week. Build a searchable library of your best teaching.

This is a 12-month game: month 1 you have 4 videos, month 12 you have 52 — and new students find you while you sleep.

A parent Googling "A/L physics wave motion Sri Lanka Sinhala" and finding your video is a warm lead who's already seen how you teach before they contact you.

Month 1 you have 4 videos. Month 12 you have 52 — and new students find you without a single extra marketing effort.

05
Understand the digital divide — online supplements, it doesn't replace

About 30% of Sri Lankan students still have no reliable internet access, particularly in rural areas. For these students, online-only isn't an option.

Online is your scaling lever, not your replacement strategy.

The hybrid model — physical class with an online version running in parallel — gives you the broadest reach. Rural students attend physically; Colombo students join online. Same session, different channels.

06
Use existing platforms to reach beyond your geography

Online listing platforms like OnlineTuition.lk, Siplo.lk (1,220+ qualified teachers), and TeacherOn.com (6,000+ SL tutors listed) have existing parent traffic. Listing on them doesn't replace your own channels — it adds to them.

Set up a profile once, keep it updated with current results, and let it work in the background.

These platforms are useful for reaching parents in districts where you have zero word-of-mouth presence yet.

A profile on an established platform reaches parents who have never heard your name. Geography becomes optional.

The teachers who built successful hybrid classes didn't figure out the tech first. They solved the teaching problem first — which students work well at home, which subjects transfer to video, which class size stays manageable — and then chose the simplest possible tech to match. Starting with Zoom before answering those questions is why most teachers stop after month two.

Identify three of your current students you think would work well online. Ask them. That conversation alone will tell you whether your subject and student type suits the format.
