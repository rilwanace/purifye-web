“I teach 6 days a week. I mark papers on Sunday. I don't remember the last time I had a full day off.”
“I'm working more hours than I ever have, but I feel like I'm getting less done.”
“I became a teacher because I love helping students. I spend maybe 60% of my time actually doing that.”

Working constantly and still feeling behind is a signal — not that you need to work harder, but that too much of your time is going to the wrong things.

01
Name your one full day off — and make it non-negotiable

Teaching six days a week and spending Sunday on admin is not dedication. It is a system that is broken. The teachers who sustain 10+ year careers without burning out almost all share one habit: they protect at least one full day where they do not teach, do not reply, and do not plan.

This single commitment changes how you structure every other day in the week. When you know Sunday is protected, you stop letting Saturday bleed into it.

Write this down: "[Day] is my full day off. No teaching, no WhatsApp, no prep." Then tell a family member or colleague — accountability makes it stick.

This single commitment changes how you structure every other day in the week.

02
Time-block your week before anyone else fills it

Most teachers have teaching hours and then everything else bleeding into the gaps. Time-blocking means deciding in advance: these hours are teaching, these are prep, these are off. If it is not in the calendar, it gets filled by whatever arrives first.

What gets scheduled gets done. What does not get scheduled gets interrupted.

Every Sunday evening, block teaching prep, marking, and your full day off before the week starts. Two 90-minute prep windows minimum.

What gets scheduled gets done. What does not get scheduled gets interrupted.

03
Set your response windows — and publish them

Every WhatsApp notification during a teaching session is a boundary failure, not a communication priority. You do not have to reply immediately. You feel like you do because you never told parents when you do reply.

Parents adjust to this faster than you expect. The adjustment starts with you, not them.

Set two 45-minute response windows. Send the announcement to all parents this week: "I respond to messages between 6-7pm and 9-10pm on weekdays. For urgent matters, call me directly."

Parents adjust to this faster than you expect. The adjustment starts with you, not them.

04
Say no to scope creep — protect what you were hired to teach

Parents ask for extras: catch-up lessons, out-of-scope subject help, marking for classes you do not teach, emergency sessions the night before a paper. Every unplanned yes adds unpaid hours and chips away at your boundaries.

Scope creep does not make you more valuable. It makes you more available, which is not the same thing.

Write down what your fee covers. Anything outside that scope is a separate conversation with a separate fee — or a polite no.

Scope creep does not make you more valuable. It makes you more available — which is not the same thing.

05
Track your total weekly hours once — the number changes everything

You do not need to change anything yet. Just count one week: teaching, prep, admin, parent messages, marking, travel, and quick replies. Add them up.

Do this audit once. It takes one week. The result stays with you.

Most teachers who feel like they are always working are clocking 55-70 hours per week. Seeing that number written down is usually the thing that makes every other strategy in this list feel non-negotiable.

Most teachers feel like they're always working because they are. The number confirms it. Once confirmed, something changes.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Name your day off — now
Write your one-day-off rule — "[Day] is my full off day — no teaching, no WhatsApp, no prep." Tell someone today
Put it in your calendar as a recurring block — Every week, all day — recurring appointments protect time better than willpower
Identify what currently fills that day — What currently gets scheduled on your "off" day? That's what you're protecting it from
~30 min
week2
Time-block before anyone else fills it
Block teaching prep before the week starts — Two 90-min windows, recurring, phone on DND — plan them Sunday evening
Set response windows and put them in the calendar — Two 45-min slots, not "when I have time" — 6–7pm and 9–10pm work for most
Block anything that doesn't get a slot, doesn't happen — If it's not in the calendar, it gets filled by whatever arrives first
~1 hour
week3
Set the boundary and hold it
Send the response window announcement to all parents — "I respond to WhatsApp 6–7pm and 9–10pm on weekdays. Urgent: call me directly"
Set teaching hours as Do Not Disturb — No replies during class — one message at the end: "I was teaching — responding now"
Write down what your fee covers — and what it doesn't — Any extra lesson, extra marking, or out-of-scope help is a separate conversation
~1 hour
week4
Protect the rest
Count your total hours for this week — Teaching + prep + admin + messages + travel — write the total down
Compare to a 50-hour week — Above 50 hours consistently means the boundaries aren't fully in place — identify the leak
Schedule one activity that has nothing to do with teaching — On your off day, this week — not "next week when things settle down"
~1 hour
After 4 weeks, you'll have:
✓
One full day off named and protected in the calendar, non-negotiable
✓
Weekly time blocks set before the week fills prep, marking, and day off blocked first
✓
Response windows published to all parents "I reply between 9–10am and 7–8pm"
✓
A scope statement written what your fee covers, what it does not
