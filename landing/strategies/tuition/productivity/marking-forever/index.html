Strategies›Education & Tuition›Improve My Productivity›Test papers and marking take forever
“I spent 4 hours on Saturday marking 30 papers. By the end I wasn't giving real feedback — I was just trying to finish.”
“I create a new test paper from scratch every month. I don't know why I don't just reuse them.”
“My students ask me for more practice papers. I want to give them more. But creating them takes as long as marking them.”

Marking and paper creation are the two most time-consuming teaching tasks — and both can be dramatically reduced with the right format changes.

01
MCQ practice tests: self-correcting in 5 minutes

A 20-question MCQ practice test can be peer-corrected in class by exchanging papers and reading answers aloud. No collection, no marking pile to take home. 5 minutes in class.

You're not compromising quality. You're removing marking time while making practice more exam-relevant.

Design your regular practice tests in MCQ format where the subject allows. O/L and A/L exam papers include MCQ sections — your practice aligns to real exam format.

You're not compromising quality. You're removing marking time while making practice more engaging for students.

02
Build a question bank — stop writing from scratch

After each test, save the questions you wrote to a master document organized by topic and difficulty. After 6 months, you have 80–100 questions per topic.

You've already written the questions. They exist in old test papers. Spend one afternoon organizing them — you'll never start from scratch again.

Future tests are assembled in 10 minutes by picking questions from the bank. The first year is investment. Every year after is return.

You've already written the questions. Spend one afternoon organizing them and you'll never write from scratch again.

03
Compile a comprehensive past paper pack — once

5–10 years of past papers, organized by topic and year, printed and bound (or distributed as PDF). Distribute at the start of the year.

A student working through a 5-year past paper pack practices every question type that will appear in the exam. That's better than anything you'd write, and it takes you one afternoon to compile.

The most useful practice material already exists. You don't need to create it — you need to curate it.

Past papers are the most reliable practice material available. They already exist, they're free, and they cover everything.

04
Marking rubrics cut structured question time in half

For structured and essay-style questions, a marking rubric defines what gets marks, how many, and for exactly what content.

A marking rubric is your own decision made once and applied many times.

Create rubrics for your 10 most common question types once. With a rubric, marking a structured question takes 60 seconds — check, tick, add up. Without one, it takes 3–5 minutes as you re-read and re-decide every time.

A rubric for 30 structured papers saves 1–2 hours of marking per test session. Across a year of monthly tests, that's 12–24 hours returned to you.

A marking rubric is your own decision made once and applied many times.

05
At 150+ students, consider a marking assistant

A final-year undergraduate or a student who recently scored well in the subject can mark MCQ and structured responses against your rubric. Pay per paper or a fixed monthly amount.

Your expertise is the teaching. The marking against a rubric is a mechanical task.

At 150 students × 4 practice tests per month = 600 papers. At your time value, that's 40–60 hours of marking. A marking assistant does it for LKR 30,000–60,000 — less than 20% of one month's revenue.

Your expertise is the teaching. The marking against a rubric is a mechanical task. Stop treating them as the same.

Your 4-week plan

Here's how to execute everything above, week by week.

week1
Build the question bank
Compile all past papers from the last 2 years — One folder, sorted by subject, year, paper — digital only
Tag each question by topic — Rough tags (Algebra, Waves, Organic) let you search instead of re-reading papers
Stop creating from scratch — Your next exam pulls from the bank — assembly, not creation
~2 hours
week2
Run first peer correction
Design one MCQ test for next class — Separate answer key — students never see it before submission
Run peer-correction in class — Each student marks the paper to their left — read answers out one by one
Note what to adjust — Disputed answers? Process feel fair? What to change next time?
~1.5 hours
week3
Cut paper creation time
Write a 5-question template per question type — Structured, essay, MCQ, data interpretation, short answer — one each
Build next month's exam from the question bank — 2–3 questions per topic, check mark distribution — under 30 minutes
Create a marking rubric for each question type — One rubric for structured, one for essay — justify marks in 10 seconds
~2.5 hours
week4
Systematize the workflow
Write a 4-step marking workflow — Read, apply rubric, write comment, record score — same every time
Set a marking time budget per student — MCQ: 90 sec. Structured: 4 min. Essay: 6 min — timer-based is faster
Calculate how many marking hours you've saved — Compare this week to Week 1 — the number justifies the bank and rubrics
~1 hour
After 4 weeks, you'll have:
✓
MCQ peer-correction running for at least one class 5 minutes in-class, no take-home marking
✓
A question bank covering 2 years of papers organised by topic, ready to use
✓
Marking rubrics created for your top question types one rubric = one decision made permanently
✓
A new exam template that populates in under 30 minutes picked from the bank, not written fresh
Marking is now a structured workflow, not an open-ended task.

The marking problem compounds. The longer you spend marking one test, the fewer tests you set, and the fewer tests you set, the more each one feels high-stakes and the more time you give it. The question bank breaks that cycle — not because it saves time on any single test, but because it changes the relationship between setting and marking entirely.

Print three past papers you've used before. Put them in one folder. Label it "Question bank." That's the start. The act of treating them as a resource rather than throwaway material changes how you'll think about marking from here.
