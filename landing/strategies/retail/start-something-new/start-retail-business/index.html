If you're sitting on a plan and can't quite pull the trigger — good, that means you're being serious about it. Here are 6 things you need to get right before and during your first month.

01
Choose what to sell — and don't try to be a mini Cargills

75–78% of grocery FMCG in Sri Lanka still flows through small traditional shops. The sillara kade isn't dying — it's the backbone of how this country shops. But the shops that struggle are the ones that try to stock everything and end up undercapitalised across all of it.

Pick one primary category first. Grocery has thin margins (3–8% net) but high daily traffic. Clothing has much higher margins (15–35% net) but needs fashion sense and inventory discipline. Cosmetics has good margins (15–30%) and growing demand. Start with one, do it well, add categories later.

Identify one thing your area genuinely lacks. A sillara kade near a school that doesn't stock stationery. A boutique where the only other option is a 2km trip to the main road. Specialisation is the most reliable path to a loyal customer base.

02
Assess your location before you sign anything

Location is the single biggest driver of foot traffic for small retail. Main-road frontage in a Colombo suburb commands LKR 100,000–160,000/month. A side-street shop nearby can be 40–60% less. The difference in traffic can justify the rent gap — but only if the foot traffic is genuinely there.

Count foot traffic at different times before signing. Stand outside and count for 30 minutes on a Monday morning, a Saturday afternoon, and an evening after 6pm. Talk to people in the area: Is there a gap? What do they currently travel elsewhere for?

Check what's within 200 metres. A sillara kade works best within walking distance of dense residential streets. A boutique needs visible access from somewhere people already go. A Cargills or Keells nearby is competition for branded FMCG but not for credit, fresh goods, or convenience.

Your first WhatsApp broadcast list is worth more than your first month of Facebook posts.

03
Get registered — it's simpler than you think

Most small shops operate without full legal compliance, and enforcement is low for micro-retailers. But having your trade license protects you from fines, enables you to open a proper business bank account, and makes supplier relationships more formal.

Start with the trade license from your Municipal Council or Divisional Council. You need a Grama Niladhari certification of the premises and your identity documents. This is the foundational document.

Get your TIN (Tax Identification Number) from the Inland Revenue Department. Required for formal business registration. If your annual turnover is likely to stay below LKR 60 million, VAT registration is not required — most small shops never reach this threshold.

Trade license + TIN is the minimum. Start there. The full compliance picture (VAT, SSCL, income tax) only becomes relevant as you grow — the Manage My Money section covers this in detail.

Register the business on week one — not when it becomes a problem in year three.

04
Plan your starting stock by category — not by feel

The capital requirements are completely different depending on what you sell. A grocery kade needs a broad but shallow stock (many SKUs, small quantities of each). A clothing boutique can start with 40–60 pieces. An electronics accessories shop can start with under Rs.100,000 of stock and still cover the common requests.

Don't overbuy on opening. Buy 3–4 weeks of estimated stock. You don't yet know what moves in your specific location. Buying 12 weeks of stock upfront ties up capital and creates dead stock risk immediately.

Use Pettah wholesale for flexibility. Cash, no minimum orders, wide range — ideal for testing what sells before committing to a distributor relationship. Pettah is your low-risk first source for almost any category.

Opening stock rule: buy 3 weeks, not 12 weeks. You don't yet know what your location sells.

05
Build your first supplier relationships

FMCG distributors visit approximately 600 outlets per sales rep on a 2-week cycle. When you open, get on their list early — the rep comes to you, you don't have to go anywhere. Initial terms will be cash (7-day) until you establish credibility; established shops typically get 14-day credit.

Start by calling the main FMCG distributor in your area. Ask for the sales rep who covers your road. They'll add you to their route. This is how 270,000+ small shops in Sri Lanka get stocked.

Build a simple supplier contact list. Name, company, phone, what they supply, visit frequency, credit terms. Even three suppliers on a contact sheet is better than relying on memory.

Credit terms start tight. A new shop typically gets 7-day cash terms. After 3–6 months of reliable payment, most distributors extend to 14-day credit automatically. Don't push for 30-day terms in your first month — build the relationship first.

The distributor rep doesn't find you — you call them, get on the route, and start the relationship.

06
Start with WhatsApp and separate your money from day one

The biggest financial mistake small retailers make isn't buying the wrong stock — it's mixing business and personal money from the first week. When the shop cash pays for school fees, groceries, and transport, you have no way to know if the shop is profitable.

Open a separate bank account for the shop before your first sale. All supplier payments out, all customer payments in. Even a basic savings account works. This single step makes profit calculation possible.

Set up WhatsApp Business for the shop on day one. Add a product catalog with photos and prices for your top 20 items. Create a broadcast list from your first customers. This is how you create repeat business before you have a following — it costs nothing and works on the phone you already have.

Hi, this is Nilu's Fashion House, Maharagama. We're open from 9am to 8pm, Mon–Sat. WhatsApp us your request and we'll confirm if we have it — we also deliver within Maharagama for orders over Rs.1,500.

Separate bank account before the first sale. Not after the first year.

Your first week in business sets the habits that run for years. Get the bank account, get the trade license, get on one distributor's route — and build your WhatsApp customer list from the very first sale. Every successful shop in Sri Lanka started with a first day. Yours is close.
