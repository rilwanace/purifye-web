Strategies›Retail Shop›Grow My Sales›I'm losing customers to bigger shops and chains
The Cargills opened six months ago and I told myself it wouldn't affect me. It has.
They're selling the same Milo for Rs.40 less. I don't know how I compete on price when they buy in bulk and I don't.
My best customer told me she still comes here for the credit and the fresh vegetables. I just need to lean into that harder.

Before you panic — the structural reality is more in your favour than it feels. Here's how to understand which customers you're losing and which ones will never leave.

01
The chains only hold 22–25% of the market — traditional trade still dominates

Despite 500+ Cargills locations, 130+ Keells, and 400+ Lanka Sathosa, modern trade holds only 22–25% of grocery FMCG volume in Sri Lanka. Traditional shops hold 75–78%. This isn't sentiment — it's measured market data. The sillara kade has been resilient through chain expansion because it serves a fundamentally different occasion.

You're not in a war you're losing. You're in a coexistence where chains win on branded FMCG weekly shopping and you win on daily top-ups, credit, fresh goods, and convenience.

Identify which specific categories you're losing. If it's branded packaged goods — that battle was already lost. Focus on what you win: fresh, loose, credit, proximity, service.

02
Nine things chains cannot replicate — and you do every day

Credit (no supermarket offers credit to individual households). Personal relationships (the owner who knows your name, family, and preferences). Hours (many kade open 6am–10pm vs chains 8am–8pm). Extreme proximity (200m vs 2km). Flexible micro-quantities (one egg, 50g chili in a newspaper cone). Local fresh produce. Speed (3-minute transaction vs 30-minute supermarket visit). Neighbourhood delivery. And the ability to put something aside for a regular customer.

List which of these you are already doing and which you've stopped doing. Shops that stop extending credit, reduce hours, and depersonalise service are the ones that actually lose customers to chains.

Double down on the ones you do best. If your credit system is strong, make it known. If your fresh produce is better, stock it prominently. If your hours are longer, advertise them.

The customer who goes to Keells on Saturday still needs daily top-ups from you. Are you there?

03
Most households shop at both — you're not losing all of their business

Research confirms that most Sri Lankan households use both formats: supermarkets for weekly branded goods top-up, local shops for daily fresh goods and immediate needs. These are different shopping occasions served by different formats.

Your customer who now goes to Keells on Saturday is not gone. She's doing a different kind of shopping trip. The question is whether she's still coming to you for daily needs — and if not, why not.

Ask your regulars directly. 'I notice you come less on weekday mornings — is there anything we don't carry that you're getting elsewhere?' This conversation reveals exactly what categories you're losing.

A supermarket cannot sell you one egg at 7pm. You can.

04
Fresh and loose goods are your permanent stronghold

Supermarkets cannot match small shops on loose goods — rice, flour, spices, dhal — where you sell in micro-quantities at prices that reflect actual purchase cost, not centrally-packaged markup. And local fresh produce sourced directly from nearby farmers is demonstrably fresher than centrally distributed supermarket stock.

If you're in grocery, build your loose goods section. These are the items supermarkets handle poorly. Display them clearly, price them visibly, and stock more SKUs than the chains — they simply can't do 50g-to-500g flexible packaging at the kade scale.

For any perishables, know your fresh sourcing story. 'This comes from the Katuneriya farm every Tuesday and Friday' is a fact no Cargills can match.

Personal relationships are the moat no loyalty programme can cross.

05
Speed is your structural advantage — use it

A typical small shop transaction takes 2–4 minutes from walk-in to walk-out. A supermarket trip — parking, basket, queue, self-checkout confusion — takes 20–40 minutes. For daily top-ups and convenience purchases, customers know this. They come to you because they're not going to queue for one can of coconut milk.

Make speed a feature, not an accident. Know where everything is. Have the most common items at the front. Don't make customers wait while you search the back room.

Add delivery for your immediate neighbourhood. A customer who WhatsApps her order at 5pm and gets it at 5:45pm has no reason to ever go to a supermarket for daily needs. You don't need a vehicle — a bike or a tuk works for a 1–2km radius.

Fresh local produce sourced Tuesday and Friday is demonstrably fresher than supermarket central distribution.

06
Personal service is the moat no chain can cross

In Sri Lankan retail, the owner-customer personal relationship is a central loyalty driver. Knowing a customer's name, family situation, preferences, and extending goodwill during hardship builds loyalty that no supermarket loyalty programme can replicate. This is documented in research, confirmed by the coexistence pattern, and visible in every successful small shop.

Track your top 20 customers by name. Know what they buy. Note when they haven't been in for a week. A WhatsApp check-in ('Hi Mrs. Wickramasinghe, we got fresh ash plantain in today — I know you usually ask for it') is worth more than any promotion.

Remember occasions. New Year greetings, a small gift during Avurudu for your best customers — these aren't expenses, they're retention investments in people who will send their friends.

Hi Mrs. Wickramasinghe, we got fresh drumstick and gotukola in this morning — I saved some knowing you usually take these on Wednesdays. Come by when you can or I can send with the afternoon delivery.

The shops losing customers to chains are usually the ones that stopped doing what made them special.

The structural reality is in your favour. Traditional trade is 75–78% of the market for a reason. Chains win on branded goods in bulk. You win on credit, proximity, freshness, and relationship. Stop competing where you lose and dominate where you win.
