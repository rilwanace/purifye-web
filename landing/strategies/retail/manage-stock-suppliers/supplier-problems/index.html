Strategies›Retail Shop›Manage My Stock & Suppliers›Distributors push me around and I can't do anything about it
The rep left a box of that new biscuit variety I didn't order and said 'just try it.' I know I'll be stuck with half of it.
During the crisis the big shops got the milk powder allocations first. I was the last to hear and the last to receive.
I pay on time, every 14 days. But I get zero preferential treatment when there's a shortage.

A single sillara kade has almost no leverage over a distributor individually. But there are specific things you can do to protect yourself and improve your position over time.

01
Understand the leverage reality — then work with it, not against it

A typical FMCG distributor's sales rep covers 600 outlets. Your shop represents roughly 0.17% of their route. You have no individual pricing leverage, no allocation priority, and no ability to refuse without simply not being served. This is the reality.

What you do have: a long-term relationship value that accumulates over years. Distributors who prioritise during shortages choose shops that have been reliable payers for 5–10 years. Loyalty in both directions is the only lever small retailers have.

Pay on time, every time. This is your primary form of leverage. A shop with a perfect payment record gets preferential treatment when allocations are scarce. A shop with late payments is the first to be deprioritised.

0.17% of a distributor's route means almost no individual leverage. Perfect payment history is your leverage.

02
Track supplier price changes to detect when you're being charged more

Distributors sometimes adjust prices between visits without explicit notice. You order the same items at the same quantities and find a higher price on the invoice. Without a record of past invoices and prices, you can't detect this — and it erodes your margin silently.

Keep the last 3 invoices from each distributor in a folder. Before paying a new invoice, compare the unit prices to the previous one. Any increase of more than 3–5% that wasn't announced deserves a call to the rep for an explanation.

Also watch for quality substitutions. Same product, different batch, different size or weight — but same price. This is a common margin-compression tactic. Compare net weight on the packaging.

Keep the last 3 invoices from each supplier — price increases without notice are common.

03
Use Pettah wholesale as both a backup source and a negotiating signal

When you tell a distributor rep 'I got this from Pettah last week at X price,' you gain a small but real piece of leverage. You're not bluffing — you can actually do it. Pettah wholesale has cash pricing, no minimum order, and often competitive prices on categories where distributors have inflated margins.

Make one Pettah run per quarter even if you don't urgently need anything. You learn what's available, you compare prices, and you build a relationship with a wholesale contact. This relationship becomes valuable the next time a distributor fails you.

For categories where you have a Pettah alternative, use it for 20–30% of your purchases. This keeps your distributor relationship alive while demonstrating that you have options.

Forced stock you didn't order is distributor risk transferred to you. Document it.

04
Learn to refuse pushed stock — politely and firmly

Distributors push near-expiry or slow-moving stock onto smaller retailers who have less bargaining power to refuse. The rep places it on the shelf and the invoice comes with the order. Refusing feels uncomfortable — they're your main supplier and you don't want to damage the relationship.

The script: 'I appreciate you bringing this, but I can't take this item this cycle — I'm already holding more than I can move before the date. I'll order it if it clears well in the area.' Polite, specific, not aggressive. Most reps accept this if you're otherwise a reliable account.

If you're consistently pressured into slow stock, escalate to the area manager, not the rep. Reps have sales targets that create this behaviour. Their manager has a relationship-quality mandate. One conversation at the right level changes the dynamic.

A Pettah relationship gives you both a backup source and a negotiating signal.

05
Compare suppliers across categories to build a multi-source position

Many product categories have more than one distributor or source. FMCG has category specialists — one for beverages, one for personal care, one for dairy. Clothing has Pettah, local manufacturers, importers. The shop that has two sources for a category has double the leverage of a shop with one.

For your top 3 product categories, identify at least 2 potential suppliers. Get price lists from both. Even if you primarily use one, having the second contact builds your fallback position.

Cross-compare key item prices quarterly. A 5% better price from an alternative supplier on your top 10 SKUs compounds significantly over a year.

Polite, specific refusals of pushed stock are accepted by most reps if you're a reliable account.

06
Document what you're forced to take — it builds your case for better terms

When a rep places stock you didn't request and it appears on your invoice, note it. Item, quantity, date, rep name. After 3–6 months, this log is evidence in a negotiation: 'In the last quarter, I was placed Rs.12,000 of stock I didn't order, of which Rs.6,000 hasn't moved. I'd like better credit terms to compensate for this.'

Keep a simple forced-stock log. Most shop owners complain about this but have no record. The rep's manager responds to specific numbers, not general complaints.

Use forced stock as leverage for better credit terms. If you're absorbing allocation risk that should sit with the distributor, you have a genuine argument for 21-day or 30-day credit terms instead of 14-day.

Forced stock you didn't order represents distributor risk transferred to you. In formal retail, this would come with a right of return. In traditional small retail, it doesn't — but documenting it gives you a negotiating basis that most shop owners never use.

The rep has a sales target. Their manager has a relationship-quality mandate. Escalate to the right level.

A single sillara kade has limited individual leverage. But perfect payment history, documented records, and an alternative source option are the levers you do have. Use them deliberately over months, not in a single confrontation. The relationship improves gradually if you play it correctly.

Your 4-week action plan

week1
Collect your last 3 invoices from your primary FMCG distributor
Compare unit prices across the three invoices — flag any items that increased more than 5%
Start a forced-stock log: any item placed without your order goes in the log with date and value
week2
Contact your main distributor rep and ask to discuss terms — specifically payment schedule alignment
Identify one alternative supplier (Pettah or a different distributor) for your top category
Practice the refusal script: 'I appreciate this but I can't take it this cycle — I'll order it if it clears well in the area'
week3
Make one Pettah visit — compare prices on your top 5 items against distributor prices
Get prices from the alternative supplier and compare to primary supplier
Calculate: how much forced stock did you accept last quarter? What is its total value?
week4
Request 21-day credit terms from your primary supplier if you've been on-time for 6+ months
Move 20% of your purchases to the alternative supplier as a test for one month
Review forced-stock log — if pattern is clear, use it as basis for a manager-level conversation
Supplier position strengthened
✓
You have an invoice tracking system, a forced-stock log, a Pettah backup relationship, and a refusal script. Your supplier position is now stronger than it was yesterday.
