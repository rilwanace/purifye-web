Retail theft by employees is common worldwide, and small shops are particularly vulnerable because the owner can't be everywhere. The fix is mostly system design, not confrontation. Here's how to make theft harder and easier to detect.

01
Establish whether you actually have a theft problem — or an accounting one

Before investigating staff, confirm that you have a genuine theft problem. Consistent shortfalls are also caused by unrecorded personal drawings, unrecorded expenses, credit credit extended without a note, errors in giving change, and stock sold at wrong prices. These are all loss sources that don't involve deliberate theft.

Spend one full week running the till personally with no staff involvement. Count rigorously at opening and close. If shortfalls disappear, the problem is staff. If shortfalls persist, the problem is your system. This establishes a clean baseline.

Calculate your shortfall over the past month. Consistent Rs.200–500 per day is Rs.4,000–10,000 per month. That's the number you're managing. Knowing the size makes the solution proportionate.

Run the till alone for one week before accusing anyone. Confirm whether the problem disappears.

02
Close the phantom sale gap — the most common staff theft method

The most common small retail staff theft method: a customer pays cash, the staff member pockets the cash without recording the sale. The product leaves the shop, the cash disappears, and there's no record. No overring, no refund — just an unrecorded transaction.

Require a written sales slip or POS record for every transaction above Rs.200. Even a handwritten pad numbered sequentially. If the number sequence has gaps, a sale was unrecorded. This single control makes phantom sales visible.

Cross-check stock levels against sales records weekly. If 10 units of an item sold according to records but inventory shows 15 units missing, 5 units disappeared without a record. This cross-check exposes phantom sales without requiring you to watch every transaction.

Phantom sales — unrecorded transactions — are the most common cash theft method in small retail.

03
Control physical access to stock — most stock theft is opportunistic

Stock theft — items taken directly, not through cash manipulation — is almost always opportunistic. If a staff member can take two biscuit packets home in their bag without anyone noticing, some will. Not because they're dishonest people in general, but because the opportunity presented itself repeatedly with no apparent consequence.

Conduct random stock counts for high-risk items. Choose 5 fast-moving items each week and count them at opening and close against sales records. Rotate which items you count so there's no pattern. Staff who know you count unpredictably steal less.

Control access to the stockroom. Only the owner and one trusted staff member enter the stock area. Other staff request items and the approved person retrieves them. This one structural change significantly reduces stock theft opportunities.

Most staff theft is not premeditated — it's opportunistic. Good system design removes the opportunity, which removes most of the problem, without requiring you to assume the worst about people or create a hostile work environment.

Random stock counts with no predictable pattern reduce theft more than any fixed system.

04
Create clear cash handling rules — and make them visible

Staff who operate under ambiguous cash rules are more likely to make 'decisions' about cash that benefit themselves. Specific, stated rules remove ambiguity and create a clear framework: any deviation is obviously wrong, which makes honest staff more careful and dishonest staff more cautious.

Create three rules and post them visibly at the till: (1) Every sale is recorded immediately. (2) Change comes from the till only — never from personal cash. (3) Till is never open without a recorded transaction.

Count the till at shift handover in front of both parties. The outgoing person counts in front of the incoming person. Both sign the count sheet. This makes any shortfall immediately attributable to a specific shift and removes ambiguity about when it occurred.

Shift handover counts with both parties present make any shortfall immediately attributable.

05
Well-treated staff with loyalty steal less — this is documented

Research on retail staff theft consistently shows a correlation between how staff feel treated and their likelihood of theft. Staff who feel respected, fairly paid, and valued in their role are significantly less likely to steal. This isn't about being naive — it's about understanding the actual drivers of the behaviour.

If you pay above minimum wage and treat staff with respect, say so directly. 'I pay you fairly and I trust you — I need you to protect this shop like it's yours.' This conversation, done sincerely, changes behaviour more than any control system.

Small gestures matter: a meal on a long day, a salary advance when someone has an emergency. A staff member who owes you goodwill is far less likely to take from you.

Well-treated, fairly-paid staff who feel trusted steal less. This is documented.

06
If you have evidence — how to handle the confrontation

If you have clear evidence of theft (consistent shortfalls that stop when the staff member is absent, a count that catches the difference in real time, or security footage), you need to address it. The goal is resolution, not humiliation.

Have the conversation privately, with the evidence in hand. 'Your till has been Rs.350 short every day this week and last week. I need you to explain this.' State the fact. Wait for the response. Do not accuse in front of other staff.

Under Sri Lankan labour law, termination for cause requires a formal inquiry process for staff employed for more than one year. For shorter-term staff, you have more flexibility. Document everything. If you pay any disputed amount as a resolution, get a signed acknowledgement. A Labour Tribunal dispute is expensive in time even when you're right.
