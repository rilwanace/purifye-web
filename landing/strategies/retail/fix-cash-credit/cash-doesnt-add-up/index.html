Strategies›Retail Shop›Fix My Cash & Credit Problems›My cash never adds up at the end of the day
I count the till at closing and it's always different from what I think I made. Sometimes Rs.200 short, sometimes Rs.500. I've stopped trying to work out why.
I can't tell if I'm making money or losing it. I sell all day and somehow there's never anything left.
My husband takes cash for petrol, I take some for lunch, the kids come after school — it all goes into the same pile.

If you can't count your daily profit, you're running blind. The most common reason is not theft — it's structural: business and personal money are mixed from the first transaction of the day. Here's how to fix it.

01
Separate business cash from personal cash — this is the first non-negotiable

The single most common cash management problem in Sri Lankan small retail is mixing business and personal money. Shop sales, family expenses, personal purchases — all from the same pile. When everything is mixed, you cannot calculate profit, cannot know if you're solvent, and cannot identify theft or error.

Open a separate bank account for the shop. All sales revenue goes in. All supplier payments go out. Personal withdrawals are recorded as 'owner drawings' — a specific, known amount taken at a specific time, not random dips throughout the day.

Set your personal drawings as a fixed weekly or fortnightly amount. Decide: 'I take Rs.15,000 per week for household expenses.' Take that amount, on the same day each week, and record it. Now you can see what the shop made versus what you lived on.

Business and personal money in the same pile means you will never know if you're profitable.

02
Do a daily cash count — it takes 5 minutes and reveals everything

A daily cash count compares what should be in the till against what is. 'Should be' = opening float + sales total. 'Is' = physical count of notes and coins. Any difference is either an error, a missed transaction, an undisclosed personal withdrawal, or theft.

Set up a simple daily cash sheet: opening float, sales today, expected closing balance, actual closing balance, difference. If the difference is consistently Rs.0, your system is working. If it's consistently positive or negative, you have a systematic issue to investigate.

Involve no one else in the closing count initially. When you're first setting up, do it yourself, alone, after closing. Once you've established a baseline you trust, you can delegate.

A consistent small daily shortfall (Rs.150–300) is usually unrecorded petty expenses — someone bought a pen, paid for a small repair, made change for a customer. A consistent larger shortfall (Rs.500+) needs investigation. Both are invisible without the daily count.

A daily cash count takes 5 minutes and makes every other financial question answerable.

03
Record sales daily — even in a simple notebook

You don't need a POS system, a spreadsheet, or any technology. You need a daily sales record. Date, total cash sales, total credit credit extended, total credit collected, expenses paid. Five columns, one line per day.

Start this notebook today. Write down last night's closing till amount as your opening float. Record every cash sale as it happens — or, if that's impractical, record the total at the end of the day based on the count. Either method works.

At the end of each week, add up the daily totals. This week's total sales. This week's total expenses. The difference is your gross profit — before you account for cost of goods, which is the next layer.

Fixed owner drawings on a fixed day eliminates the random dips that cause unexplained shortfalls.

04
You cannot know profit until you track your buying cost

Many small retailers track sales but not purchases. They see Rs.85,000 in sales for the month and feel like they had a good month — but don't know that Rs.72,000 of that was cost of goods, leaving Rs.13,000 before overheads. Knowing your buying cost is the only path to knowing your actual profit.

Record every supplier payment as an expense. Date, supplier name, amount paid. Monthly, sum these up. Your gross profit is: monthly sales − monthly cost of goods bought. For most small groceries, expect 10–20% gross margin. For clothing, 30–50%.

If your sales are Rs.150,000 and your supplier payments are Rs.130,000, your gross margin is 13%. Before rent, electricity, and wages. That tells you whether your prices are right and whether your turnover is high enough.

Gross profit = sales minus what you paid suppliers. Calculate this monthly. It's your north star.

05
A fixed opening float stops till confusion before it starts

Every shop day should start with the same amount of change in the till — your opening float. If Monday starts with Rs.2,000 in the till, every Monday should start with Rs.2,000. Any deviation is a signal of something unrecorded the night before.

Decide your opening float amount based on typical change requirements. For most small shops, Rs.1,500–3,000 covers morning change needs. Count it out from the previous day's closing and set it aside before you bank or take anything personal.

Put the banking amount in a separate envelope or container each evening. Anything above float + owner drawings goes to the bank account. Not tomorrow, not next week — same evening or next morning. This discipline prevents 'float creep' where the till gradually becomes an informal petty cash reserve.

Break-even is the daily sales number below which you're subsidising the shop with personal savings.

06
Track every business expense — even the small daily ones

Unexplained cash disappearance is usually accumulated untracked expenses: the R.O. water delivery, the bag of ice, the electrician who fixed the fan, the staff lunch when they worked late. These are real costs. Without a record, they show up as 'mystery shortfall' in your till count.

Keep a receipt box or a simple expense notebook. Every business expense goes in — amount, what it was for, date. Even Rs.50 for elastic for repacking. This isn't bureaucracy, it's knowing where your money is going.

At month end, your total expenses tell you your true net profit position. Gross profit minus all tracked expenses equals your net profit. If that number is negative or near zero, you now know which expenses to address first.

Nilu's Fashion House daily count: Opening float Rs.2,000. Sales today Rs.18,400. Credit collected Rs.1,200. Expenses paid Rs.650 (electricity Rs.200, bags Rs.450). Expected close Rs.20,950. Actual count Rs.20,950. ✓ Clean.

Supplier invoices in a monthly folder is the minimum record-keeping for tax compliance and profit calculation.

The daily cash count is the most important 5-minute ritual in small retail. Without it, you can't distinguish profit from loss, error from theft, business spending from personal spending. Start it tonight. The picture becomes clear within two weeks.

week1
Open a separate bank account for the shop before the next banking trip

Count the till at closing tonight — write opening float, today's sales (estimate), closing count, difference
week2

Record every expense in the expense notebook, even Rs.50 petty cash
week3
After two weeks, review: is the daily difference consistently zero? If not, what's the pattern?
Calculate this week's gross profit: sales total minus supplier payments
Set your opening float amount and prepare it every evening before closing
week4
Build the first monthly P&L: sales, cost of goods, gross profit, overheads, net profit
Calculate break-even: monthly overheads ÷ gross margin % = monthly sales needed
Review: is your daily average above break-even? If not, what's the gap?
