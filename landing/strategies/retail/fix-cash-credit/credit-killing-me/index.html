My neighbour's family hasn't paid their credit tab in four months. How do I ask for it without destroying the relationship?
I write it in the book but half my customers think I've forgotten. I haven't. It's just awkward.
I add up the credit book sometimes and it's more than my entire weekly sales. That money is just... gone.

60–80% of sillara kade sales in Sri Lanka run on credit. Credit is a genuine competitive advantage — supermarkets can't offer it. But without a system, it quietly transfers your working capital to your customers. Here's how to manage it.

01
The credit book is your credit ledger — treat it like one

Most shops keep an credit book but don't use it systematically. Entries are incomplete, dates are missing, and the total owed per household is never calculated. A book with 50 names but no running balance is a record of trust extended, not a receivables management tool.

Keep a separate page per credit customer. Date, item, amount — on every transaction. Running balance visible at the bottom. This single change makes the conversation with the customer factual ('Your balance is Rs.3,450 as of Tuesday') rather than vague ('You owe something').

Calculate your total credit book monthly. Sum every customer's balance. Write it on the inside cover with the date. This is your receivables position — and the trend tells you whether credit is growing or shrinking.

02
Set a credit ceiling per household — and stick to it

Without a ceiling, credit grows to the point where the customer can't realistically clear it. A household with Rs.8,000 on their tab is more likely to avoid the shop than to come in and pay. The ceiling protects both you and the customer.

Set a personal ceiling for each household based on what they can reasonably repay in one month. For a household spending Rs.8,000–12,000 per month in your shop, a Rs.3,000–4,000 ceiling is roughly one week's exposure. When a customer reaches their ceiling, that day's purchases are cash only.

Be direct, not apologetic: 'Your tab is at Rs.3,200 which is where I need to keep it — let's clear this first and I'll keep your book open after that.' Customers who are genuinely good payers understand. Customers who push back are the ones whose tabs you should be most concerned about.

03
Collect on salary day — not randomly

The best time to collect credit is within 48 hours of a customer's salary. Private sector workers get paid around the 25th–1st of each month. Government workers around the 25th–26th. Customers who intend to pay will do so when they have cash — that window is 2–3 days long.

WhatsApp your credit customers on or just after their salary date. Not a demand — a reminder: 'Hi Chamara, just a note that your balance is Rs.2,450 — if you can clear some this weekend we'd appreciate it. We have new stock in too if you want to come by.'

Do not let more than 45 days pass without a collection attempt. A 45-day-old balance has a reasonable chance of recovery. A 90-day-old balance is significantly harder. The older the debt, the lower the effective recovery rate.

Salary-day collection is the highest-probability collection window — 48 hours after the 25th.

04
The social pressure that makes credit work — and what breaks it

Credit works because of social proximity. The customer lives 200 metres away, sees the shop owner at the temple, is known by name. This proximity creates natural accountability. The customers who abuse credit are almost always ones where this social proximity has broken down — they've moved, had a falling out, or simply think you won't pursue it.

Community standing is your most powerful collection tool. 'I know your mother, I know where you work' is never said directly — but it's the implicit guarantee behind every credit transaction. Don't discard this. It's more effective than any legal mechanism for small amounts.

For customers who repeatedly ignore collection attempts, stop the credit first. 'I can't extend more credit until your balance is cleared — I hope you understand.' Delivered neutrally, without confrontation, this is both effective and professionally unimpeachable.

Credit done well is a competitive moat. Credit done loosely is your biggest cash drain.

05
Keep your cash position separate from your credit receivables

A critical accounting error: counting credit balances as part of your financial strength. The money you're owed on credit is not cash — it's a receivable with uncertain collection probability. A shop with Rs.40,000 in the till and Rs.80,000 in credit does not have Rs.120,000 in assets. It has Rs.40,000 in cash and Rs.80,000 in promises.

In your weekly financial summary, separate: cash on hand, cash in account, and credit outstanding. The first two are real. The third is an estimate of what you expect to recover — at best 80–90% if your book discipline is strong, potentially much less.

Never use credit receivables to justify overspending on stock. Buy based on cash, not on the expectation that credit will be collected.

06
Credit done well is a competitive moat — not just a risk

The shops that manage credit best are not those that eliminate it — they're those that systematise it. A well-run credit system with clear ceilings, proper records, and salary-aligned collection is a permanent competitive advantage. No supermarket can match this. No app can replace this for the household that genuinely needs this week's groceries and can pay next Friday.

Communicate your credit system to new customers as a service, not just a convenience. 'We keep an credit book for regular customers with a small credit limit — it helps families manage their month.' This positions it as a feature of your shop rather than an informal favour.

Consider a simple credit card — a physical card per household recording their ceiling and current balance. Customers who carry it come more frequently. The card makes the credit relationship feel formal and fair, which increases prompt payment.

Credit is the service that built traditional Sri Lankan retail and the reason the sillara kade survived 500+ supermarket locations. Managed properly, it's your strongest loyalty tool. Managed loosely, it's your biggest cash drain.
