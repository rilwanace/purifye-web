Strategies›Retail Shop›Improve My Productivity›I can't find or keep good staff
I've had seven staff members in two years. Every time I train someone properly they leave for a supermarket job or to go abroad.
The ones who stay, I'm not sure I can trust. And I can't pay supermarket wages when I'm a one-shop operation.
I'm scared to leave even for a few hours in case something goes wrong or the wrong decision gets made.

Staff retention in small Sri Lankan retail is a genuine structural challenge — you compete with formal employers who offer EPF/ETF, fixed hours, and career paths. But the shops that keep good staff do specific things differently.

01
Pay slightly above the going rate — the cost of turnover is higher than the wage gap

The minimum wage for retail and allied sector workers was raised to LKR 21,000/month in 2024. Many small shop owners pay at or slightly below this. The challenge: supermarkets and formal retail pay Rs.25,000–35,000 with EPF/ETF contributions. Your best candidates know this.

If you can afford Rs.2,000–3,000 above minimum wage, do it. The cost of staff turnover — training time, lost productivity during the transition, the owner's increased workload — typically exceeds Rs.50,000 per replacement. Paying Rs.24,000 vs Rs.21,000 per month (Rs.36,000 per year extra) is cheap insurance against a Rs.50,000+ disruption.

Register your staff for EPF/ETF if you have more than one employee. Under Sri Lankan law this is mandatory for businesses with more than one employee. More practically: staff who are EPF-registered feel more secure and are less likely to leave for a formal employer primarily because of the security.

02
Train properly in the first two weeks — ambiguity causes most early exits

Most small retail staff departures in the first 3 months are caused by one of three things: unclear expectations, feeling untrusted, or feeling unsupported when they make a mistake. None of these require more money — they require better onboarding.

Create a written list of what the staff member is responsible for. Open/close procedures. Cash handling rules. What to do if a customer complains. How to handle credit requests. Spend the first two days going through this together, not just telling them to watch what you do.

Set clear boundaries for decisions they can make independently. 'You can give 10% discount on items I've marked. You cannot give credit without checking with me first. If a supplier comes and I'm not here, take their card and say I'll call back.' Clarity removes the anxiety that makes people leave.

03
Give your best staff member a title and genuine responsibility

In Sri Lankan culture, being addressed as 'shop manager' rather than 'shop boy' or 'worker' matters significantly. Recognition of status is a retention tool. The shops that keep good staff long-term almost always have one staff member who has been explicitly elevated — trusted with keys, handling small supplier interactions, or managing the counter independently.

After 3–6 months, identify your best-performing staff member and give them a formal role. Even 'Head Cashier' or 'Shop Supervisor' printed on a simple business card creates identity and loyalty. Tell them clearly: 'You're the person I trust to run this shop when I'm not here.'

This person becomes your succession plan for absences. When you can trust one staff member to run the shop for a half-day, you begin to get your life back.

Ambiguous expectations in the first two weeks cause more early exits than pay.

04
Hire from your community — referrals have a lower turnover rate

The best small retail hires come from community networks — family referrals, temple or mosque connections, school leavers from families you know. These hires have a social accountability layer that direct hires from Ikman or word-on-the-road don't. If someone's aunt referred them, leaving without notice has social consequences.

When you need staff, ask your three best customers first. 'I'm looking for a reliable person for the shop — do you know anyone in the area who's looking?' This network-sourced hire understands your shop culture before they start.

Consider part-time or school-leaver hires for first exposure. A school leaver at Rs.15,000/month for Saturday–Sunday and school holidays builds your roster without full-time cost. You evaluate them over 3–6 months before committing to full-time.

A staff member given a title and genuine responsibility stays longer than one who 'just works in the shop.'

05
Systematise the most common decisions so staff can make them without you

The owner-dependency problem in small retail is structural: every non-routine decision comes to the owner because there's no documented process for anything. Staff can't make decisions because they don't know what the rules are. You can't leave because every exception requires your judgement.

Write down the answers to the 10 most common questions staff ask you. 'Can we give a discount?' — yes, up to 10% on marked items. 'What if a supplier calls?' — take the name and number. 'What if the till doesn't balance?' — note the amount and call me. These 10 answers, posted in the back room, eliminate most interruptions.

Review the list quarterly — add any new recurring question. Over time, you're building an operations manual for your shop. This is what allows a business to function without the owner at the centre of every decision.

10 written decision rules in the back room eliminate most of the interruptions that keep you in the shop.

06
Small recognitions create loyalty that no competitor can easily buy

In small retail, the owner-staff relationship is personal by nature. This is both a vulnerability (you're affected by their personal problems) and an advantage (you can show care that a corporate employer cannot). Staff who feel genuinely valued by their employer leave less often and work harder.

Three low-cost retention tools that work in Sri Lankan small retail: A meal together on festival days. A small bonus — even Rs.1,000 — at Avurudu and Christmas. Public acknowledgement when something goes well: 'Kumari handled the shop perfectly on Thursday when I was away.'

When a staff member has a family emergency or health issue, be flexible first. 'Take the day, sort yourself out, we'll manage.' This generosity at a vulnerable moment creates loyalty that a Rs.3,000 salary premium at another shop can't overcome.

EPF-registered staff feel more secure and are less likely to leave for a formal employer solely for the security.

Good staff stay when they're paid fairly, treated with respect, trained properly, and given real responsibility. The cost of turnover exceeds the cost of paying Rs.2,000 above minimum wage — calculate this once and it changes your hiring philosophy permanently. Your best hire is probably already in your community network.
