Strategies›Retail Shop›Manage My Money Better›I don't know if I'm actually making money
I'm selling every day. The stock moves. But I feel like I'm always short. I honestly don't know if I'm profitable.
My margins are fine on paper but when I add up rent, electricity, and my own salary I'm not sure the numbers work.
I've never done a proper profit calculation. I wouldn't even know where to start.

Not knowing whether your shop is profitable is more common than you think — and completely fixable without accounting software. Here's a step-by-step method any shop owner can do in an afternoon.

01
Gross margin — the one number that tells you if your prices are right

Gross margin is the percentage of your selling price that's profit before overheads. For a grocery that buys Milo for Rs.450 and sells for Rs.490, gross margin is 8.2%. For a boutique that buys a dress for Rs.1,800 and sells for Rs.3,500, gross margin is 48.6%.

Know your gross margin for each product category, not just overall. Groceries typically earn 5–15% gross margin. Clothing 30–50%. Cosmetics 20–40%. Electronics accessories 20–35%. If you're below these ranges consistently, your buying prices are too high or your selling prices are too low.

Calculate gross margin for your 10 best-selling items this week. Take the selling price, subtract what you paid for it, divide by selling price. This is your category margin signal. One afternoon of this calculation tells you more about your business than a year of guessing.

02
A one-page monthly P&L — do this once and the picture becomes clear

A profit and loss statement for a small retail shop has four lines: Sales, Cost of Goods (what you paid suppliers), Gross Profit (Sales minus COGS), and Overheads (rent, electricity, wages, everything else). Net Profit is Gross Profit minus Overheads.

Build this for last month using your records. If you don't have exact numbers, estimate: sales from your daily count notebook, supplier costs from your invoices or invoice total. Even approximate numbers make the structure clear.

The result tells you three things: Is gross margin healthy? Are overheads proportionate to revenue? And is there anything left after both? Most shop owners who do this for the first time find either a margin problem (buying too expensive or selling too cheap) or an overhead problem (rent that consumes too much of gross profit).

If your net profit doesn't cover a fair salary for yourself, the shop is not yet a viable business.

03
Know your break-even — the daily sales you need just to survive

Break-even is the daily sales figure where you cover costs exactly — not profit, just survival. Knowing it tells you whether last Tuesday was a good day or a bad one. Most shop owners don't know this number.

Calculate it: monthly fixed costs (rent + electricity + wages + all overheads) ÷ gross margin %. Example: Rs.45,000 fixed costs ÷ 15% gross margin = Rs.300,000 monthly sales needed to break even = Rs.10,000 per day (25 trading days).

If your daily sales average is below break-even, you're losing money — not just not growing. This single calculation creates urgent clarity about whether you need to increase sales, cut costs, improve margin, or some combination.

Break-even formula: Monthly overheads ÷ Gross margin % = Monthly revenue needed. Divide by 25 trading days for your daily target. If you're averaging below this, you're subsidising the shop with personal savings.

04
Some categories are carrying your shop — others are dragging it down

In most mixed retail shops, 2–3 categories generate the majority of profit while 1–2 categories barely cover their floor space. Identifying which is which allows you to double down on the right categories and reduce or eliminate the unprofitable ones.

For each product category you carry, estimate: monthly sales, monthly buying cost, gross profit. A category doing Rs.30,000 sales with Rs.27,000 buying cost contributes Rs.3,000 gross profit. A category doing Rs.20,000 sales with Rs.14,000 buying cost contributes Rs.6,000. The second category is far more valuable despite lower sales.

Eliminate or reduce categories with gross profit below Rs.2,000 per month. That floor space, capital, and management time is better deployed on your strongest categories.

05
Include your own salary as a cost — or you're fooling yourself

The most common small retailer accounting error: calculating profit without including the owner's time and labour as a cost. If you work 12 hours a day, 6 days a week in the shop, that labour has a market value. A comparable hired manager would cost Rs.35,000–50,000 per month. If your 'profit' is Rs.25,000, you're actually running at a loss when your labour is priced correctly.

Decide on a fair monthly salary for yourself and include it in your overhead calculation. What would you need to pay someone to do what you do? Include that as a cost in your P&L.

If profit remains after this adjusted calculation, the shop is genuinely viable. If not, the shop is a self-employment mechanism that may need price increases, volume growth, or cost reduction to become a real business.

A category with less than Rs.2,000 gross profit per month is consuming floor space and capital that could work harder.

06
Review your financials quarterly — not annually, not never

Annual reviews miss the mid-year problems. Monthly reviews are too frequent for a solo shop operator to sustain. Quarterly is the right rhythm: deep enough to catch trends, infrequent enough to actually do it.

Set four dates on your calendar: end of March, end of June, end of September, end of December. On each date, spend two hours: update your P&L for the quarter, compare to the previous quarter, check your break-even against actual daily sales, review the credit book total.

The pattern across four quarters tells you whether the business is improving, stable, or declining. Three quarters of declining gross margin needs action — price review, supplier negotiation, or category change. Three quarters of improving net profit means your decisions are working.
