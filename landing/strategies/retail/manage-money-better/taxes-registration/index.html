Most small retailers in Sri Lanka operate with partial or full registration gaps. The risk is real but manageable — and the cost of sorting it out is much lower than you think. Here's exactly what you need and in what order.

01
The two documents every shop must have — and how to get them

The municipal trade license is the foundational compliance document for any retail business. Without it, a council inspector can issue a fine and in theory order you to close. In practice enforcement is inconsistent — but the risk is real and the fix is straightforward.

To get your trade license: visit your Municipal Council or Divisional Secretariat with your NIC, proof of premises (lease agreement or owner consent letter), and a Grama Niladhari certification of your address. The fee varies by council (typically Rs.1,500–5,000 depending on shop size and location). Processing takes 2–4 weeks.

Get your TIN (Tax Identification Number) from the Inland Revenue Department at the same time. Required for formal registration. In Colombo, the IRD head office in Chittampalam; outstation shops can apply at the nearest IRD regional office. Bring your NIC and business details. This is free and processed immediately.

Trade license and TIN are the gateway documents for every formal business opportunity.

02
VAT — you almost certainly don't owe it unless you're doing Rs.60M+ annually

VAT registration is compulsory only if your annual taxable supplies exceed Rs.60 million — LKR 5 million per month. This is a very high threshold for small retail. The vast majority of independent sillara kade, boutiques, and cosmetics shops in Sri Lanka will never approach it.

If your annual turnover is below Rs.60 million, you do not need to register for VAT. You cannot charge VAT, and you cannot claim input VAT credits on your purchases. For most small retailers, this is actually an advantage — your effective prices are lower than VAT-registered businesses.

The threshold went from Rs.80M to Rs.60M in 2024. If you're approaching Rs.5M per month in sales, get professional advice on whether voluntary registration is worthwhile for your category before the mandatory threshold triggers.

03
Income tax — what you actually owe as a small retailer

Sole proprietors pay income tax on net business profit above LKR 1.2 million per year (the personal exemption threshold as of 2024/25). The marginal rate is 6% on the first Rs.500,000 above exemption, rising to 12%, 18%, 24% on higher bands.

If your net profit (after all costs including owner salary) is below Rs.1.2M per year, you owe no income tax. For a shop making Rs.80,000 net profit per month, annual net is Rs.960,000 — below the threshold.

If your net profit is above Rs.1.2M annually, you need to file a return with the IRD each year. The filing deadline is November 30 for the previous assessment year (April–March). Late filing attracts penalties. This is the one area where professional advice is genuinely worth the cost.

SSCL at 2.5% kicks in at Rs.12M annually — many mid-sized shops do reach this.

04
SSCL — the 2.5% social security levy most small shops don't know about

The Social Security Contribution Levy (SSCL) at 2.5% applies to businesses with quarterly turnover above Rs.3 million (Rs.12M per year). This is lower than the VAT threshold. A shop doing Rs.1.2M per month is above this threshold and technically liable.

SSCL applies to your gross revenue — not profit. A shop with Rs.15M annual turnover owes Rs.375,000 in SSCL per year, even if profit is modest. This is a significant obligation that many small retailers are unaware of.

If you're approaching Rs.1M per month in sales, consult an accountant about your SSCL position. Filing and payment happens quarterly. The penalties for non-filing are disproportionate to the levy itself and are worth avoiding.

05
Keep simple records now to make tax filing simple later

You don't need an accountant to maintain adequate records for small retail tax compliance. You need three things: a sales record (daily totals), a purchase record (supplier invoices filed monthly), and an expense record (all other costs). These three combined give you everything needed to file an accurate return.

File every supplier invoice in a monthly folder. Not loose, not mixed with personal papers — monthly folders. This takes 5 minutes when each invoice arrives and saves hours at tax time.

If your business grows to the point of complexity, an accountant for annual filing costs Rs.15,000–30,000 for a small retailer. This is worth it at the point where SSCL or income tax is clearly owed. Before that point, the IRD's own guidance notes are sufficient.

06
Compliance opens doors — bank loans, formal supplier terms, expansion

The practical benefit of getting your registrations in order goes beyond avoiding fines. Banks require a registered business with a TIN for an SME loan. Formal supplier distributors prefer registered businesses for credit terms and allocations. A business without trade license and TIN is capped in how far it can grow.

Get your trade license and TIN this quarter if you don't have them. These are the gateway documents for every subsequent formal business interaction. The Rs.2,000–5,000 license fee and half a day at the government office is the cheapest business investment you'll make.

Once compliant, open a dedicated business bank account. A bank account in the business name, even a basic savings account, is the foundation of a formal financial identity. It enables banking credit history, formal transactions, and eventual loan applications.

Monthly invoice folders take 5 minutes to maintain and save hours at tax time.

Compliance is not a burden — it's infrastructure. A trade license, a TIN, and a business bank account are the gateway documents for every formal opportunity: bank loans, supplier credit, expansion. Get them in order this month. The fines for ignoring them are disproportionate; the cost of getting them is minimal.

week4
Set filing deadline reminders: income tax return by 30 November each year
