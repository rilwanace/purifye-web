Cash flow problems in small retail are almost always predictable — because the Sri Lankan seasonal calendar is predictable. Once you map the peaks and troughs, you can plan your capital position instead of reacting to it.

01
Map your annual cash flow pattern — peaks, troughs, and payment due dates

Cash flow problems don't happen randomly. They happen at predictable intersections: high stock purchase months (before Avurudu, before school opening) combined with slow collection months, combined with fixed payment obligations. Mapping these identifies when you'll be squeezed before it happens.

On a 12-month calendar, mark: when supplier payments are largest, when rent is due, when seasonal stock purchases happen, and when sales and cash collection are highest. The months where large outflows coincide with slow inflows are your cash crunch months.

For most small Sri Lankan retailers: March and November are high-outflow months (Avurudu stock buying, Christmas stock buying). May and January are high-outflow months (post-peak supplier payments). These are predictable — you can save during April and December peaks to cover them.

02
Save 20% of April and December profits for the months that follow

April Avurudu and December Christmas are cash-generation months. May and January are cash-consumption months — post-festival lull, supplier payments still flowing, lower sales. The shops that survive the lull with capital intact are those who set aside peak earnings instead of spending them immediately on new stock or personal drawings.

In the last week of April, calculate your Avurudu profit. Set aside 20% in a separate account or envelope labelled 'May buffer.' Don't spend this except for essential business obligations. Same in December for January.

This one habit prevents 80% of the post-peak cash crunch that forces shop owners to take personal loans or supplier credit at unfavourable terms.

03
Stagger your supplier payment schedule to smooth cash outflows

If multiple supplier payments fall in the same week, that week will always be cash-tight regardless of your sales level. Staggering supplier payment due dates spreads the burden across the month and makes cash flow management significantly easier.

Ask different suppliers for different payment cycles. Grocery distributor on the 10th, clothing supplier on the 20th, miscellaneous on the 30th. Most suppliers accept this without objection — they care that you pay, not which day.

Align your largest supplier payment to the 3rd–5th of the month — after the month-end salary spike drives cash into your till, but before mid-month lull. This timing uses your strongest cash position to settle your largest obligation.

04
Overbuying stock is the most common cash trap — recognise it before it happens

The most common reason small retailers run out of cash is not low sales — it's capital tied up in unsold stock. Rs.80,000 of stock sitting on shelves is Rs.80,000 that can't pay rent, wages, or a cash opportunity purchase. Overbuying happens when distributor pressure, bulk discounts, or festival optimism leads to purchasing beyond what the shop can sell at the required pace.

Set a maximum stock value rule for your shop. Based on your selling velocity, how much total stock should you carry at any one time? For most small shops this is 3–4 weeks of sales at cost. More than this is capital inefficiency.

Before placing any large order, calculate: current stock value + order value = new total. If new total exceeds your maximum, either reduce the order or clear existing stock first.

05
Build a Rs.15,000–30,000 cash reserve — your emergency buffer

A shop with zero reserve operates with no margin for error. One unexpected expense — a broken fridge, a bad debt, a slow week — creates immediate financial distress. A modest reserve of 2–3 weeks' fixed costs changes this from a crisis to a manageable event.

Set a reserve target of Rs.15,000–30,000 depending on your monthly overheads. This lives in a separate account, untouched except for genuine emergencies. Build it over 3–6 months by setting aside a fixed Rs.2,000–5,000 per month from profits.

Once the reserve is built, replenish it when used before taking owner drawings. The reserve is not optional savings — it's infrastructure for business stability.

Every day of extra supplier credit is free working capital — negotiate it deliberately.

06
Supplier credit terms are cash flow tools — use them deliberately

14-day supplier credit means you have 14 days between receiving stock and paying for it. During those 14 days, you may have already sold that stock and collected cash. This float is a real cash flow benefit — but only if you use it deliberately rather than letting it slip.

Push for 21-day credit terms with your primary supplier once you have 6 months of on-time payments. Every extra day of credit is extra free working capital. The conversation: 'I've paid on time for 6 months. I'd like to extend our terms to 21 days to help me manage month-end cash flow.'

Never take a cash discount offer unless the discount exceeds your opportunity cost of the capital. A 2% discount for paying in 7 days instead of 14 sounds good. But if that 7-day window is a cash-tight period, you're borrowing the discount at a high cost.

Never use credit receivables to justify a large stock purchase. Buy based on cash, not promises.
